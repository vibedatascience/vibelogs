Van Gogh, in thirteen paintings

Vincent van Gogh signed his paintings with just his first name, when he signed them at all. He said he disliked hearing his last name mispronounced, and that a signature was not needed anyway because everyone would know the work by how it looked. He was right about that. He was born in 1853 in Groot-Zundert in the rural south of the Netherlands, and before painting he failed at two careers: six years as an art dealer with Goupil and Company starting at age 16, then an attempt to become a preacher like his father, which ended with a mission post among miners in the Borinage in Belgium. At 27 he decided to become an artist. He taught himself by copying drawing manuals and engravings, working through Bargue's three volumes of exercises three times. He then produced roughly 900 paintings in ten years, wrote about 900 letters, around 700 of them to his brother Theo, sold about one painting in his lifetime, and died in 1890 at 37. The whole arc is visible if you walk through the work in order. That is what this page does.

Nuenen, 1883 to 1885

The Potato Eaters

He moved back in with his parents in Nuenen and painted the people around him: 28 compositions of weavers alone, plus farm laborers and peasant heads. He wanted to be a painter of rural work in the tradition of Millet, whose Sower he had copied over and over. Theo, by then a successful dealer in Paris, was paying his bills, and Vincent was irked that his brother would not take the work. He wrote to him: "You have never ever sold one single painting of mine, either for much or for little, and in fact you haven't even tried."

The Potato Eaters, from April 1885, was his answer, a deliberate showpiece. At two by four feet it was large for him, took a month, and required more studies than anything he had done. He was reading Charles Blanc on color theory, and instead of putting complementary colors side by side he mixed reds and greens into what he called coloristic grays, which is why the picture is so dark. He did not want the figures academically correct. "My great longing is to learn to make incorrectnesses, changes in reality, so that they may become, yes, lies if you like, but truer than the literal truth." He wanted the people to look like they had dug the earth with the same hands they put in the dish.

Antwerp, winter 1885 to 1886

Skull with a burning cigarette

Theo kept telling him the work was too dark and that he should go look at paintings. A trip to Amsterdam to see Frans Hals lit something: "My palette is thawing," he wrote after seeing orange set against blue. He enrolled at the academy in Antwerp that winter, clashed with his instructors, and left little that survives. This small canvas is the exception, probably a joke at the expense of academic drawing exercises, which had students copy skeletons endlessly. It is the first painting of his that feels like a personality rather than a program.

Paris, 1886 to 1888

Self-portrait with grey felt hat

In March 1886 he showed up in Paris without warning. He sent Theo a note at the gallery that said, in effect, meet me at the Louvre. Theo had a one bedroom apartment, so they moved to Montmartre and lived together for two years. The timing mattered. 1886 was the year of the final impressionist exhibition, and Vincent went and saw Seurat, Signac, Degas, and Monet. His palette went from brown to full color in about a year, helped by something practical: new industrial pigments had just arrived, sold in John Goffe Rand's invention, the squeezable metal paint tube.

He took classes at Cormon's studio, became fast friends with Toulouse-Lautrec, and hung around Pere Tanguy's paint shop, where artists traded canvases for materials and where he first dug through stacks of Japanese woodblock prints. He and Theo collected the prints, and Vincent copied them the way he had once copied Millet, calling himself a musician playing a composition written by someone else. Too poor to pay models, he painted himself more than twenty times in Paris. This one from the winter of 1887 to 1888 shows him bending pointillism his own way, with strokes that radiate around the head instead of sitting in a neutral grid. City life wore him down. By his own account he was close to alcoholic by the time he left, and he knew it.

Arles, 1888 to 1889

Sunflowers

In February 1888 he took the train south to Arles. He had wanted to go to Japan, could not, and settled on Provence as the nearest thing, with a plan to found a colony of artists living and working together the way he imagined Japanese artists did. He had met Gauguin in Paris in November 1887 and swapped two sunflower paintings for one of Gauguin's Martinique canvases, so sunflowers were already tied to Gauguin in his head. Theo made Gauguin a deal: free lodging with Vincent in exchange for one painting a month. When Gauguin agreed, Vincent bought up yellow paint and made the sunflower series to decorate the guest room, yellow on yellow on yellow, a technical dare as much as a decoration.

Look closely and most of the flowers are dead or dying, dry seed heads with the petals gone. A Dutchman painting flowers inherits the 17th century tradition where blooms are reminders of mortality, and the seed heads carry the other half of the thought, since each one is full of next spring's sunflowers.

Cafe Terrace at Night

Painted in September 1888 on the Place du Forum. It is his first night sky on canvas, done on site without using any black. He wrote to his sister Willemien that night is "more richly colored than the day." He kept thinking about starry skies as something more than scenery, writing that shining dots in the sky should be as reachable as the black dots on a map of France. The cafe still exists and trades on the painting.

The Yellow House

He rented four rooms in this house on Place Lamartine for the colony, his "studio of the south." Gauguin arrived in late October 1888 and lasted nine weeks. They drank, went to the brothel, and painted the same sitters side by side, but their methods clashed. Gauguin painted from imagination and pushed Vincent to do the same; Vincent needed the thing in front of him. By late December it fell apart in what one lecturer called a perfect storm: the two argued constantly, Gauguin talked about leaving for the tropics, and a letter arrived from Theo saying he had proposed to a woman in Paris, which put Vincent's one reliable support in question.

The Bedroom

His own room in the Yellow House, painted in October 1888 while he waited for Gauguin. He told Theo the flat planes of color were meant to express rest. He liked the composition enough to paint it three times; this is the first version. The walls were originally violet and have shifted toward blue because the red component of the pigment was unstable and faded, a problem that runs through his late work, since several of the new industrial reds he loved were fugitive.

Self-portrait with bandaged ear

On the night of 23 December 1888 he cut off part of his left ear, wrapped it, and delivered it to a woman named Rachel at the local brothel at 11:30 pm. The police found him the next morning barely alive. Gauguin telegraphed Theo and left Arles; the two painters never met again, though they kept writing to each other. The only source for the story that Vincent had threatened Gauguin with a razor is Gauguin, who had reasons to tell it that way.

Vincent painted this a few weeks later, back at his easel, in a winter coat and fur cap with a Japanese print on the wall behind him. The painting reads as a statement that he was still working. Within months the neighbors petitioned to have him confined.

Saint-Remy, 1889 to 1890

Irises

He committed himself voluntarily to the asylum of Saint-Paul-de-Mausole in Saint-Remy in May 1889. He was suffering hallucinations and blackouts and said so plainly. When he was lucid he painted, sometimes in the asylum garden and sometimes out in the fields with a staff member along. Irises came out of his first week there. In 1987 it sold at auction for 53.9 million dollars, at the time the highest price ever paid for a painting. He never knew a market like that existed for him.

The Starry Night

Painted in June 1889, partly from the view through his barred window and partly from memory. The village is invented and the cypress is enlarged. He had been circling both ideas for a year. On the cypresses he wrote that month: "It astonishes me that no one has yet done them as I see them. Beautiful as regards lines and proportions, like an Egyptian obelisk." On the stars he had written: "Just as we take the train to get to Tarascon or Rouen, we take death to reach a star." He grew up around funerary cypresses in cemeteries, so the tree that connects the village to the sky in this picture is doing exactly what that sentence describes. He considered the painting a failure, an abstraction pushed too far. It is now the most reproduced painting he made.

Almond Blossom

The last year of his life was also the year recognition arrived. He was invited to show with Les Vingt in Brussels in January 1890, given four meters of wall, and one painting sold there, The Red Vineyard. The critic Albert Aurier published the first serious article on him that same month, calling him a terrible, maddened genius. Vincent objected mainly to the title, which framed him as "the isolated one." He had wanted a brotherhood, not isolation. Theo relayed that Monet called his paintings the best in a Paris exhibition that spring.

Also in January 1890, Theo's son was born and named Vincent Willem after his uncle. Van Gogh painted this branch of almond blossom against a sky blue background to hang above the baby's bed. Almond trees flower earliest in Provence, so the picture is about a new life arriving before spring. The family kept it, and it anchors the Van Gogh Museum collection today.

Auvers-sur-Oise, May to July 1890

Portrait of Dr. Gachet

He left the asylum in May 1890, tired of being isolated, and moved to Auvers, close enough to Paris to see Theo but out of the city, under the care of Dr. Paul Gachet, a homeopathic doctor, amateur painter, and collector who treated artists. Vincent diagnosed himself with "a melancholic aversion to life" and was pleased that Gachet, like him, had red hair; he joked they were a brotherhood of redheads. In his last 70 days he painted around 70 canvases. He painted Gachet in June with what he called "the heartbroken expression of our time." In 1990 the portrait sold for 82.5 million dollars, again a world record.

Wheatfield with Crows

In his final month he painted more than a dozen canvases in an unusual double square format, wide and low. This is the most famous of them and is often called his last painting, though the family believed the actual last one was Tree Roots, a tangle of gnarled trunks that circles back to drawings of tormented roots he made in his very first years. Everything here reads as an ending anyway: a storm sky, three paths that resolve nothing, crows over the wheat. He wrote that these wheatfields expressed "sadness and extreme loneliness," and also that the country air was doing him good. Both sentences are from the same period.

On 27 July 1890 he went out to the fields, set his easel against a haystack, and shot himself in the chest. He died two days later with Theo at his side. Emile Bernard, who came for the funeral, described the scene: Vincent's last canvases nailed to the walls around the coffin like a halo, sunflowers and yellow flowers laid on it, his easel, folding stool, and brushes set on the floor beside it. Gachet tried to speak and wept too much to finish. Theo died six months later, and the brothers are buried side by side in Auvers. It was Theo's widow, Jo van Gogh-Bonger, who spent the next three decades publishing the letters and placing the paintings until the world caught up.
