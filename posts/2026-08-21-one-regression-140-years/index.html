One regression, 140 years of machines

The problem never changes on this page. In 1886 Francis Galton measured 928 adult children and their parents and asked a plain question: given the parents' height, how tall will the child be? Pediatricians still ask it. The mid-parental height formula on a growth chart today is this regression with the numbers rounded.

So hold the problem fixed and change only the machine. Each era below shows who would have fit the line, on what hardware, and how long it would have taken at two dataset sizes: Galton's 928 rows, and a million rows. Every number is either sourced or marked as an estimate.

The data and the answer

Galton 1886, 928 children. x is mid-parent height (father, plus mother scaled by 1.08, averaged). Circle area is count. Line is least squares: child = 23.94 + 0.646 × midparent. Slope under 1 is the "regression to the mean" that gave the method its name.

The fit needs five running sums over the rows: count, Σx, Σy, Σx², Σxy. Per row that is two multiplications and four additions. Then one division for the slope and one for the intercept. That is the whole job, in 1886 and now.

n   = 928
Sx  = 63390.0     Sy  = 63186.1
Sxx = 4333017.5   Sxy = 4318042.0
b   = (n*Sxy - Sx*Sy) / (n*Sxx - Sx*Sx) = 0.6463
a   = (Sy - b*Sx) / n                   = 23.94
GOTCHA · GALTON DID NOT RUN THIS
Galton never computed least squares. He binned the 928 children into a two-way table of heights, which has only 102 occupied cells, smoothed the counts, and drew the line through the column medians by eye. Legendre (1805) and Gauss (1809) had the least squares math; Karl Pearson (1896) first applied it to Galton's kind of data. The 1886 card below estimates what the arithmetic would have cost had he done it the modern way.

Time to fit, by year

Wall-clock time for one least squares fit, log scale. Eras that could not hold the data at all are left off the line. Solid is 928 rows, dashed is 1,000,000 rows.

Two things stand out. The first is that the arithmetic stopped being the bottleneck around 1954. From the IBM 650 onward the line itself takes well under a second of compute, and what you wait for is the card reader, the tape drive, the batch queue, or a row cap in a spreadsheet. The second is that the last fifty years of the chart are a story about access, not speed. In 1975 a million-row fit required a department, a mainframe, and an overnight queue. In 1985 it was not possible on a desk at any price, because the spreadsheet stopped at 8,192 rows. In 2007 it took three seconds in Excel. In 2024 it runs on a phone, and for small tables you can paste the numbers into a chatbot and get the slope without owning a computer you can name.

How the times are computed

For human and desk calculator eras, time per row is the time for two multiplications of three-digit numbers plus the additions, taken from period accounts of computing bureaus. For punched-card eras, time is set by card throughput, since a 928-row job is read-bound. For mainframes and later, time is max(compute, I/O, queue), using published instruction rates and media speeds. Costs are list price or monthly rental at the time, with today's dollars from the BLS CPI (2024 base). Where a machine physically could not hold the data the card says so instead of inventing a number.
