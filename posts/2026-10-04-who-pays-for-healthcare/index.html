Who pays for US healthcare?

The US spent $5.3 trillion on healthcare in 2024, about $15,500 for every person. Very little of that bill is paid by the patient at the counter. How much lands on you depends mostly on how you are covered.

what you pay
Premium
Monthly fee to stay insured, used or not.
Deductible
What you pay each year before insurance starts paying.
Copay
Flat fee per visit or prescription, like $30.
Coinsurance
Your percent of the bill after the deductible, like 20%.
Out-of-pocket
Deductible + copays + coinsurance. Premiums are not included.
Out-of-pocket max
Yearly cap on those costs. After it, the plan pays 100%.
ways to be covered
Employer plan
Insurance through a job. The employer pays most of the premium.
ACA marketplace
Plans you buy yourself on Healthcare.gov or a state site. Income-based tax credits lower the premium.
Medicare
Federal plan for 65+ and some disabled people. A: hospital, B: doctors, D: drugs.
Medicare Advantage / Medigap
Private plans that bundle Medicare (Advantage) or cover its gaps (Medigap).
Medicaid / CHIP
Federal-state coverage for low-income people. CHIP covers kids in families just above the Medicaid limit. Little or no premium.
who pays
Payer
Who writes the check to the hospital: you, an insurer, Medicare or Medicaid.
Sponsor
Who funds that payer in the end: households, employers or taxpayers.
Subsidy / tax credit
Government money that lowers what you pay, such as ACA credits.
Uncompensated care
Care a hospital gives and never gets paid for.
DSH payments
Extra Medicaid money for hospitals with many low-income and uninsured patients.

01 / three words

Every dollar is a premium, an out-of-pocket cost, or someone else's money

insurance cost
Premium
A fixed monthly charge to stay covered. You pay it whether you see a doctor or not, like a gym membership.
out-of-pocket cost
What you pay when you use care
The deductible, the copay at each visit, and coinsurance, which is your percentage of the bill after the deductible.
someone else
The rest of the bill
Your employer's share of the premium, Medicare and Medicaid, ACA tax credits, workers' compensation and clinics.
deductible: you pay 100%→coinsurance: you pay a share→out-of-pocket maximum→insurer pays 100%

03 / the bill moves

What people pay barely moved in 20 years. What others pay did.

Inflation-adjusted, 2003 against 2023. For people on Medicare, the amount they pay is about the same, around $4,200. Their premiums are higher and their out-of-pocket costs lower, partly because Part D started covering prescriptions in 2006. The government's share per Medicare enrollee rose by about $3,000.

Why ACA plans cost more in total. Before 2014, insurers selling to individuals could charge more or say no based on health, and did not have to cover common services. The ACA required them to take everyone and cover ten essential benefits, which raised the total, and federal tax credits picked up most of the increase.

2026 change. The larger tax credits added in 2021 expired at the end of 2025. Many marketplace buyers saw premiums rise for 2026, some lost credits entirely, and others moved to cheaper plans with higher deductibles. Enrollment is still near its record high.

04 / 65 years of the health dollar

Patients paid almost half in 1960. Now they pay a tenth.

National health expenditures by who wrote the check, 1960 to 2024. "Other" is the VA, the military, workers' compensation, public health and research. Medicaid includes CHIP. Hover or tap for any year.

Medicare and Medicaid started in 1966. Their arrival is the step in the chart. Out-of-pocket spending kept growing in dollars (from about $730 per person in 1960 to $1,632 in 2024, both in 2024 dollars) while it shrank as a share, because everything else grew much faster.

05 / who funds it

Count premiums and taxes, and households pay about a quarter

The chart above follows the payer. CMS also follows who ultimately funds the payer: your premium share and payroll taxes feed private plans and Medicare. On that view, households funded $4,277 per person in 2024. That is the "$4,300 out of their own pocket" figure from the video.

06 / the uninsured

The cheapest bucket on paper, and the least counted

Uninsured Americans as a share of the population, 1987 to 2024 (CMS). ACA marketplaces and Medicaid expansion began in 2014.

of uncompensated care happens in hospitals, which must screen and stabilize emergencies first
$19B
federal Medicaid DSH payments in FY2021 to hospitals serving many uninsured and Medicaid patients

Uncompensated care is treatment a hospital or doctor delivers and never gets paid for. Federal law requires nearly every hospital with an emergency department to screen and treat life-threatening cases before asking about payment. Nobody publishes a full count of what that costs, and the $19 billion in DSH payments is only part of it.

07 / timeline

The laws that moved the bill

In every bucket, what people pay themselves is less than what their care costs. The difference is paid by an employer, by taxpayers, or by a hospital that never gets paid.
