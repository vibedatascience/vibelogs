The jobs number, from first principles

Once a month the US government counts how many more people are on payrolls than the month before. For September 2026 the count was 29,000. This page covers what that number is, why it leads the front page, how to read it, and how it compares with 87 years of months.

01 / what it is

A headcount of paid jobs, minus last month's headcount

The Bureau of Labor Statistics surveys around 120,000 businesses and government agencies about the pay period that includes the 12th of the month. Add up the jobs, compare with the month before, and you have the headline.

Farm workers, the self-employed and household staff are left out. A second survey, of about 60,000 households, produces the unemployment rate. The two surveys come out together and sometimes disagree.

what households feel
  • It comes first. It is published about three weeks after the survey week, before most other data for the same month.
  • It is broad. It covers every industry and every state, with a sample big enough to report sector detail.
  • The Fed is in a bind. It raised rates on Sep 16 to a 3.75% to 4% range to fight inflation. Weak hiring argues for the opposite.
  • Markets react. On Oct 2, stock futures rose and Treasury yields fell, as traders read the miss as a reason for the Fed to hold in October.
  • It became political. In August 2025 the BLS commissioner was fired the day a weak report came out.

03 / how to read the headline

Three comparisons do most of the work

September's gain against the forecast, recent averages, the 2019 pace, and Fed estimates of breakeven job growth.
  1. Against the forecast. 29k against 84k is a 55k miss, which is why the headlines called it weak.
  2. Against breakeven. Breakeven is the job growth needed to keep unemployment flat as the labor force grows. With immigration down, the St. Louis Fed puts it at 15k to 87k a month for 2026, and the Dallas Fed estimated about zero for late 2025. By this test, 29k is soft but sits inside the range.
  3. Against revisions and unemployment. July and August were cut by 60k combined, and unemployment rose to 4.2%. Both point the same way as the headline.
What to make of it: hiring has slowed to roughly the pace a slower-growing labor force needs, and unemployment is starting to drift up while the Fed is raising rates.
