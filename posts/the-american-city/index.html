Preface: How to Use This Book

MIT’s American Urban History is a reading seminar. There are no recorded lectures; the course lives in its readings — a carefully built mix of encyclopedia overviews, primary sources written by the people who ran (and suffered) the American city, and interpretive essays by historians arguing about what it all meant. This book was written to stand in for the missing lectures: each chapter corresponds to one week of the syllabus, teaches the assigned readings in depth, situates them in the wider historiography, and reproduces genuine excerpts from the public-domain primary sources so you can hear the period speak in its own voice.

Each chapter follows the same architecture. Learning objectives tell you what you should be able to do afterward. The narrative sections teach the week’s material, treating every assigned reading — its argument, its context, and why the instructor put it on the list. Excerpts in the margins of the narrative are real, verified passages from the primary sources. Key concepts boxes isolate the ideas that recur across the course. Timelines and tables anchor the chronology. Key terms collect the vocabulary, and discussion questions are written seminar-style, in the spirit of the course’s weekly reflections — several would serve directly as reflection-paper prompts.

The course’s central intellectual move is worth stating up front, because every chapter performs it: the American city is best understood through its institutions. Political machines, police departments, health boards, school systems, transit companies, park commissions, housing authorities — these are where the abstract forces of urbanization, industrialization, immigration, and reform became concrete arrangements of power, money, and daily life. Watch, across the chapters, how each institution is invented, captured, contested, professionalized, and reformed. The pattern repeats; noticing it is the point of the course.

A note on sources. Where the syllabus assigns public-domain texts — Engels, Plunkitt, Riis, Addams, George, DuBois, Olmsted, the tenement-house reports — this book quotes them directly and encourages you to read the originals, nearly all of which are freely available on Project Gutenberg or the Internet Archive (links in the bibliography). Where the syllabus assigns copyrighted scholarship — the Oxford Encyclopedia articles and essays by Merton, Hays, Peiss, Katz, Lepore, and others — this book teaches their arguments in original prose, with brief fair-use quotation, and directs you to the originals for the full experience. This companion supplements the readings; it does not replace them.

Chapter 1 · Week 1

Thinking About Cities and History: An Urbanizing World

How humanity became an urban species in barely a century, why the American city of 1840–1960 is the laboratory for this course, and what two classic texts—Kingsley Davis on urbanization and Louis Wirth on urbanism—teach us about cities as demographic facts and as ways of life.

Learning Objectives

  • Define urban history as a field and explain why this course approaches American cities through their institutions—political machines, police, schools, courts, prisons, and public authorities—during the period from roughly 1840 to 1960.
  • Distinguish, following Kingsley Davis (1965), between urbanization (the rising proportion of a population living in cities) and urban growth (the increase in the absolute number of city dwellers), and explain why the difference matters.
  • Describe Davis’s account of urbanization as a finite, S-shaped historical process tied to industrialization, mortality decline, and rural-to-urban migration.
  • Summarize Louis Wirth’s theory of urbanism (1938): how size, density, and heterogeneity generate a distinctively urban way of life marked by anonymity, segmental roles, and secondary contacts.
  • Evaluate the strengths and limits of both arguments as frameworks for studying the American city.

Prologue: A World Turned Inside Out

In 1850 Chicago was a muddy lake port of just under 30,000 people. By 1890 it held 1.1 million—a thirty-seven-fold increase within a single working lifetime. A person born on a farm in that era could plausibly end her days in a metropolis of elevated trains, electric light, tenements, ward bosses, and skyscrapers, none of which had existed when she was young. Nothing in prior human experience had prepared people, or their governments, for change at this speed and scale.

Chicago was extreme but not unique. It was one instance of the conversion of humanity from a rural to an urban species. For virtually all of the roughly 5,500 years that cities have existed, they were small islands in an overwhelming sea of villages and farms. Then, in little more than a century—concentrated between the mid-nineteenth and mid-twentieth centuries in Europe and North America—whole societies urbanized. Before 1850, no society on earth was predominantly urban. By 1900, one was: Great Britain. By the 1960s, every industrial nation was. Today, more than half of all human beings live in cities, a threshold the world crossed around 2007.

This chapter sets up the two questions that will run through the entire course. First, the demographic and historical question: how and why did societies urbanize, and what kind of process was it? For this we turn to Davis’s classic 1965 essay “The Urbanization of the Human Population.” Second, the sociological question: what does city life do to people—to their relationships, their psychology, their communities? For this we turn to Louis Wirth’s 1938 essay “Urbanism as a Way of Life,” arguably the single most influential article ever written in urban sociology. Together they give us the conceptual vocabulary for everything that follows.

What Is Urban History—and Why Institutions?

Urban history is the study of cities in time: how they grew, how they were governed and misgoverned, how people lived in them, and how they changed. But a course can’t study everything, and this one makes a deliberate choice. Rather than survey the American city in general, we will examine it through a series of institutions—durable, organized structures through which urban society channels power, delivers services, and imposes order. Our cast of characters includes the political machine, the police department, the public school, the courts, the prison, and the public authority.

Why institutions? Three reasons. First, institutions are where the abstract forces of urbanization become concrete: “migration” and “density” are analytical categories, but the precinct house, the ward organization, and the schoolhouse are where millions of Americans actually encountered them. Second, institutions leave records—charters, budgets, investigations, scandals, reform crusades—that let historians reconstruct how power actually worked. Third, and most important, institutions are how cities responded to explosive growth. Every institution we will study was invented or radically transformed in the nineteenth century because older, informal ways of running a community collapsed under the weight of numbers. A village of 3,000 could keep order with a constable and a night watch; a city of 300,000 could not. As Louis Wirth put it in 1938, urban conditions favor “the predominance of formal social control” over the informal controls of the small community. This course is, in large part, the history of that formalization.

Hence our chronological focus, roughly 1840 to 1960. The starting point marks the moment when American urbanization began its steep climb: in 1840 barely one American in ten lived in an urban place. The endpoint marks the era when the classic industrial city—dense, transit-based, centered on downtown—began to dissolve into the decentralized metropolitan regions of the automobile age, and when many of its signature institutions (the machine, the big-city police force, the urban school system) entered crises from which they never fully recovered. Between those dates lies the making of urban America.

Key Concept: Institutions

In this course, an institution is a durable, organized structure of rules, roles, and resources through which a society gets collective work done—policing, schooling, adjudicating, punishing, building. Institutions outlive the individuals who staff them, and they change slowly, which is why historians study them: they carry the past into the present. Urban institutions are especially revealing because most were improvised under pressure, as cities grew faster than anyone knew how to govern them.

Kingsley Davis and the Urbanization of the Human Population

Kingsley Davis (1908–1997) was among the most influential American demographers of the twentieth century: a president of the American Sociological Association, the first sociologist elected to the National Academy of Sciences, credited with coining the term “population explosion,” and a key developer of demographic transition theory. From the 1950s onward, first at Columbia and then at Berkeley’s International Population and Urban Research center, Davis and his collaborators compiled the first systematic worldwide data on city populations. His September 1965 article in Scientific American, “The Urbanization of the Human Population,” distilled that project into a sweeping argument about where cities came from and where they were going.

Davis opens with a claim of evolutionary novelty:

Urbanized societies, in which a majority of the people live crowded together in towns and cities, represent a new and fundamental step in man’s social evolution. Although cities themselves first appeared some 5,500 years ago, they were small and surrounded by an overwhelming majority of rural people… Before 1850 no society could be described as predominantly urbanized, and by 1900 only one—Great Britain—could be so regarded.

— Kingsley Davis, The Urbanization of the Human Population (1965)

Ancient cities existed for millennia, but urbanized societies—societies in which most people are urban—are astonishingly recent. Everything about modern urban life that feels permanent is, on the timescale of human settlement, brand new.

Urbanization Versus Urban Growth

Davis’s most durable contribution is a distinction that sounds technical but changes how you see everything. Urbanization, he insists, properly refers to the proportion of a total population concentrated in urban settlements, or to a rise in that proportion—a ratio. Urban growth, by contrast, is the increase in the absolute number of people living in cities. The two usually move together, but they need not: “cities can grow without any urbanization, provided that the rural population grows at an equal or a greater rate,” as Davis wrote. Conversely, a country can urbanize with slow city growth if its countryside is emptying out.

Why does the distinction matter? Because the two processes have different causes, different limits, and different futures. Urbanization, Davis argues, is a finite process—“a cycle through which nations go in their transition from agrarian to industrial society.” A proportion cannot exceed 100 percent; long before that, it stops climbing. Urban growth, on the other hand, “has no inherent limit”: cities can keep swelling through natural increase even after a society is fully urbanized. Much confusion about cities—in 1965 and now—comes from mistaking one process for the other.

The Attenuated S-Curve

Plot the urban proportion of a nation’s population over time, and you get what Davis called “a curve in the shape of an attenuated S.” Urbanization starts slowly, bends sharply upward during early industrialization, then flattens as the proportion passes 50 percent and “falters, or even declines, when the proportion urban has reached about 75 percent.” In the United Kingdom, the swiftest rise in the share living in large cities came between 1811 and 1851; in the United States, between 1820 and 1890. And Britain, the world’s pioneer, illustrated the ceiling: its urban proportion was actually slightly higher in 1926 (78.7 percent) than in 1961 (78.3 percent). The S-curve made urbanization legible as a historical episode with a beginning, a middle, and an end—one that the advanced countries were, by 1965, nearly through. Where Did the Urbanites Come From?

If the urban share of population rose, the new city people had to come from somewhere. Davis works through the logical possibilities—reclassification of growing villages, higher urban natural increase, or migration from the countryside—and eliminates the first two. Reclassification mattered little, and the nineteenth-century city was a demographic sink: it killed people faster than it bred them. London’s mid-century water supply, Davis notes, was drawn from wells and rivers that drained cesspools and graveyards; around 1850 life expectancy was about 36 years in London and 26 in Liverpool and Manchester, against 41 for England and Wales as a whole. Urban birth rates ran well below rural ones besides. The arithmetic left only one possible engine: “the only real source for the growth in the proportion of people in urban areas during the industrial transition was rural-urban migration.” The industrial city was built by people who left the farm—pushed off the land as agricultural productivity rose, pulled to town by higher wages in manufacturing, commerce, and services, which need land only as a site, not as an instrument of production.

Hold on to this point: it is the demographic foundation of the whole course. The American city of 1840–1960 was a city of newcomers—from the American countryside, from Ireland, Germany, Italy, and Poland, and, in the Great Migration, from the rural South. Its institutions were newcomers’ institutions: machines that traded services for immigrant votes, police forces recruited from and set against particular ethnic neighborhoods, schools charged with “Americanizing” the foreign-born. Davis explains where the strangers came from; the rest of the course examines what cities did with them, and to them.

The Developing World Is Not Repeating History

The second half of Davis’s essay turns to the underdeveloped countries of the 1960s, where his distinction pays off spectacularly. Cities in Asia, Africa, and Latin America were exploding—growing about 4.5 percent a year, on pace to double every fifteen years, far faster than nineteenth-century European cities ever grew. Yet these countries were not replaying the Western experience. Their city growth was driven less by rural-urban migration than by sheer natural increase: modern public health had slashed urban death rates while birth rates stayed high. In his Costa Rica calculation, only about 20 percent of urban growth between 1927 and 1963 was attributable to urbanization proper; some 44 percent stemmed from general population increase alone. Meanwhile the countryside was not emptying, as Europe’s had, but filling up—Venezuela’s farm population rose 11 percent in the 1950s even as Caracas quadrupled. City growth had become “unhinged from economic development.” Davis’s stark conclusion: “The problem is not urbanization, not rural-urban migration, but human multiplication.” He chided planners for treating population growth “as something to be planned for, not something to be itself planned”—a provocation that reflects the population-control politics of the 1960s, and one worth debating today.

Key Concept: The Demographic Logic of the Industrial City

Nineteenth-century cities had death rates so high and birth rates so low that they could not sustain their own populations. Every growing industrial city therefore depended on a continuous stream of rural and foreign migrants. This single demographic fact shaped almost every institution this course examines: a city of perpetual strangers needed new machinery of politics, order, and assimilation.

Louis Wirth: Urbanism as a Way of Life

Davis tells us how many people moved to cities; Louis Wirth asks what happened to them when they got there. Wirth (1897–1952) was himself one of Davis’s migrants twice over: born in the small German village of Gemünden, he emigrated to the United States at fourteen and eventually landed at the University of Chicago, where he took all three of his degrees and spent nearly his whole career. He belonged to the second generation of the Chicago School of sociology, the circle around Robert Park and Ernest Burgess that treated booming, polyglot Chicago as a social laboratory and pioneered the empirical study of neighborhoods, immigrants, gangs, and ghettos—Wirth’s own first book was The Ghetto (1928), a study of Jewish immigrant life. His essay “Urbanism as a Way of Life,” published in the American Journal of Sociology in July 1938, attempted something the Chicago School’s case studies had not: a general theory of what makes urban life distinctive.

Wirth begins from a paradox that echoes forward to Davis: the modern world is urban to a degree that mere population counts understate, because the city “is the initiating and controlling center of economic, political, and cultural life that has drawn the most remote parts of the world into its orbit.” Urbanism—the urban way of life—radiates beyond city limits. Yet sociology, he complains, had no adequate definition of the city from which such a theory could be built. He supplies one in a single famous sentence: “For sociological purposes a city may be defined as a relatively large, dense, and permanent settlement of socially heterogeneous individuals.” Everything else in the essay is deduced from the three variables packed into that definition: size, density, and heterogeneity.

Size, Density, Heterogeneity

From large numbers, Wirth derives the signature texture of urban social relations. When a settlement grows beyond the point where everyone can know everyone, personal acquaintance gives way to a world of strangers. Urbanites may interact with more people than villagers do, but they know a smaller fraction of those they meet, and know them less deeply. Relationships become what sociologists call secondary rather than primary contacts:

Characteristically, urbanites meet one another in highly segmental roles… The contacts of the city may indeed be face to face, but they are nevertheless impersonal, superficial, transitory, and segmental. The reserve, the indifference, and the blasé outlook which urbanites manifest in their relationships may thus be regarded as devices for immunizing themselves against the personal claims and expectations of others.

— Louis Wirth, Urbanism as a Way of Life (1938)

Note the phrase “segmental roles.” The grocer, the streetcar conductor, and the police officer each encounter you in one narrow slice of your life; no one but your intimates encounters the whole person. Wirth—borrowing from the German sociologist Georg Simmel’s 1903 essay on the metropolis and mental life—saw both liberation and loss in this. The urbanite gains “a certain degree of emancipation or freedom from the personal and emotional controls of intimate groups,” but loses the spontaneous self-expression and sense of belonging of an integrated community, risking the condition Durkheim called anomie.

From density, Wirth derives specialization and segregation. Packed together, people differentiate: land uses sort themselves, occupations multiply, and populations separate by income, ethnicity, and taste until “the city consequently tends to resemble a mosaic of social worlds in which the transition from one to the other is abrupt.” Density also means constant friction among strangers with no sentimental ties—and therefore, in the line already quoted above, the turn to formal mechanisms of order: the clock, the traffic signal, the ordinance, the police. From heterogeneity, finally, Wirth derives instability and fluidity. Mixed populations break down rigid caste lines, encourage social mobility, and produce a tolerant, relativistic, secular outlook; but they also leave individuals affiliated with many partial, overlapping groups rather than one encompassing community, so that the individual counts politically and socially only “as he acts through organized groups.” Figure 1.3 summarizes the whole schema.

Significance and Criticism

The power of Wirth’s essay lies in its economy: three variables, one definition, and a whole civilization’s psychology seemingly deduced from them. For decades it defined the field—it is among the most cited articles in the history of sociology—and it remains the sharpest statement of the idea that the city is not just a place but a distinctive way of life. But read it critically. Herbert Gans, studying Boston’s Italian West End in the 1950s, found “urban villages” of dense kinship and lifelong acquaintance thriving in the heart of the metropolis, and argued in 1962 that ethnicity, class, and life-cycle stage—not settlement size or density—shape social life. Claude Fischer’s subcultural theory (1975) turned Wirth partly on his head: far from dissolving social ties, urban scale generates them, providing the critical mass that lets subcultures—artistic, ethnic, religious, deviant—flourish as they never could in a small town. For urban historians, Wirth is best used not as settled truth but as a hypothesis: a checklist of what growth, density, and diversity might do to a community, to be tested against the record of actual cities.

For this course, one Wirthian insight matters above all. If urban life dissolves the informal controls of family, church, and neighborhood, then something must replace them. Wirth’s answer—formal institutions of social control and mass organization—is precisely the terrain we will spend the semester exploring. The machine organized the votes of strangers; the police disciplined them; the schools socialized their children; the courts and prisons processed those who fell afoul of the new order; the public authorities built the infrastructure that held the whole agglomeration together. Wirth wrote the theory; American cities, between 1840 and 1960, lived the experiment.

Key Concept: Urbanization vs. Urbanism

Keep the vocabulary straight. Urbanization (Davis) is a demographic process: the rising share of a population living in cities. Urbanism (Wirth) is a cultural and social condition: the way of life characteristic of cities, which can spread far beyond city boundaries. A society can be highly urbanized while pockets of it resist urbanism—and, as Wirth stressed, urbanism can pervade the countryside of an urbanized society long after urbanization itself has leveled off.

Two Lenses, One Course

Read together, Davis and Wirth give us complementary lenses. Davis supplies the macro view: urbanization as a measurable, finite, worldwide transition, driven by industrialization and migration, with the United States of 1840–1960 sitting on the steepest stretch of the S-curve. Wirth supplies the micro view: what that transition felt like from inside, and why it demanded new forms of social organization. Both essays bear the marks of their moments—Wirth’s of Depression-era Chicago, Davis’s of Cold War population anxieties—which is itself a lesson: theories of the city are historical documents too. As we move through the institutions of the American city in the chapters ahead, keep asking the two questions these authors posed. Where did the people come from, and in what numbers? And what did living among multitudes of strangers do to them—and what did they build to cope?

Discussion Questions

Chapter 2 · Week 2

Broad Arcs I: Urbanization and Industrialization, 1790–1929

How a rural republic of seaport towns became, within five generations, a nation of industrial metropolises — and how that transformation remade not only the American economy but the American self.

Learning Objectives

  • Trace the arc of American urbanization from the mercantile "walking city" of 1790 to the majority-urban nation recorded by the 1920 census.
  • Explain how the transportation revolution — turnpikes, canals, and above all railroads — reordered the hierarchy of American cities and knit them into a national market.
  • Summarize the arguments of David Schley and Jonathan Rees on the relationship between industry, commerce, and city growth in the periods 1790–1870 and 1880–1929, and identify what changed between the two eras.
  • Describe Warren Susman's account of the shift from a producer culture to a consumer culture, including his famous distinction between a "culture of character" and a "culture of personality."
  • Connect economic and demographic change to institutional change — the new organizations, buildings, and cultural forms (corporations, department stores, skyscrapers, advice manuals) that cities generated in this period.

Opening: Two Ceremonies, One Century Apart

On October 26, 1825, cannon fired in relay down the length of New York State. Governor DeWitt Clinton had boarded a canal boat at Buffalo to travel the newly completed Erie Canal — 363 miles of hand-dug waterway that critics had mocked as "Clinton's Folly" — and the guns announced his progress toward New York Harbor, where he would pour a cask of Lake Erie water into the Atlantic in a "wedding of the waters." The ceremony celebrated a piece of infrastructure, but what it really announced was a new kind of American city: one whose fortunes depended less on the ocean at its front door than on the continental interior behind it.

Eighty-eight years later, on April 24, 1913, President Woodrow Wilson pressed a button in Washington and lit up the Woolworth Building in lower Manhattan — at 792 feet and 60 stories, the tallest building in the world, a title it would hold until 1929. Its owner had made his fortune not in shipping or steel but in five-and-dime retail, selling inexpensive goods to millions of urban consumers. A clergyman soon dubbed the neo-Gothic tower the "Cathedral of Commerce." Between Clinton's canal boat and Woolworth's electric tower lies the story of this chapter: the long, entangled rise of urbanization and industrialization in the United States, and the transformation of American culture that came with it.

The Mercantile Era and the Walking City, 1790–1840

The United States of 1790 was overwhelmingly rural. The first federal census counted 3,929,214 people, and only about 5 percent of them lived in "urban" places — settlements of 2,500 or more. Just five cities held more than 10,000 residents: New York, Philadelphia, Boston, Baltimore, and Charleston. All five were Atlantic seaports, and their economic logic was mercantile: they existed to move goods between an agricultural hinterland and overseas markets, and to house the merchants, clerks, artisans, sailors, and laborers who made that exchange possible.

Historians call these early settlements walking cities. Before mechanized transportation, the practical size of a city was set by how far a person could walk in under an hour, so cities were compact — rarely more than two miles across — and densely mixed. Wealthy merchants lived near the wharves and countinghouses where they worked; artisans lived above their shops; rich and poor, workplace and home, crowded together in ways that later Americans would find unrecognizable. Social distance existed, but spatial segregation by class and function had barely begun. The walking city is a useful baseline for this course: nearly every institutional development we will study — transit systems, zoning, suburbs, downtown business districts — can be understood as part of the long unbundling of the walking city's compact mixture.

The Transportation Revolution

What broke the mercantile pattern open was a revolution in transportation. In the first decades of the nineteenth century, states and private investors poured money into "internal improvements": turnpikes, steamboat routes, canals, and eventually railroads. The Erie Canal was the spectacular success. Authorized by New York State in 1817 at a cost of roughly $7 million, it connected the Great Lakes at Buffalo to the Hudson River at Albany, and through it, to New York City. The effect on freight costs was staggering — canal transport could cut overland shipping costs by as much as 95 percent, and toll revenue exceeded the state's construction debt within a year of opening. The canal secured New York's position as the nation's premier port and commercial capital, and it spawned a string of boomtowns along its route: Albany, Syracuse, Rochester, Buffalo.

The canal's success set off a competitive scramble. Rival seaports — Philadelphia, Baltimore, Boston — feared being cut off from western trade and launched canal and railroad projects of their own; Baltimore's answer, the Baltimore and Ohio Railroad (chartered 1827), inaugurated the American railroad age. Here is a theme worth underlining for a course on institutions: American cities were not passive beneficiaries of economic change. They were actors — municipal governments, merchant elites, and state legislatures deliberately invested in infrastructure to capture trade, in what historians have called a competitive "urban mercantilism." By 1860 more than 30,000 miles of railroad track crossed the country, and the railroad map had redrawn the urban map. Inland cities with no navigable water access could now grow explosively; the most dramatic case was Chicago, a village of about 4,470 people in 1840 that reached nearly 30,000 in 1850 and, as the railroad hub of the continent, 298,977 by 1870.

The numbers tell the larger story. New York City grew from 33,131 people in 1790 to 123,706 in 1820 and 813,669 by 1860. Nationally, the urban share of population climbed from 5.1 percent in 1790 to 19.8 percent in 1860. Cities were growing far faster than the country as a whole, fed by two streams: migrants leaving the American countryside and immigrants arriving from overseas, above all from Ireland and the German states after the mid-1840s.

Reading: David Schley, "Industry, Commerce, and Urbanization in the United States, 1790–1870"

The first assigned reading, by historian David Schley — author of a prizewinning study of the Baltimore and Ohio Railroad's relationship to its home city — synthesizes this early period for the Oxford Encyclopedia of American Urban History (2019). Schley's central claim is that commerce, industry, and urbanization were not separate stories but a single braided process. The eighty years from 1790 to 1870 brought dramatic economic and demographic change, and cities grew faster than the nation as a whole precisely because they sat at the center of that change: they were the places where commercial networks, new transportation systems, and new methods of production converged and reinforced one another.

Two features of Schley's account deserve emphasis. First, he resists the assumption that industrialization simply caused urbanization. In this period the causality often ran the other way: commercial cities generated the capital, labor markets, and demand that made early manufacturing possible. Much early industry was itself urban and artisanal — the reorganization of craft work in workshops, the "sweating" of garment production, the growth of milling, meatpacking, and machine-building tied to a city's trade. Even the famous New England textile mills at places like Lowell, sited at rural waterpower, were financed by Boston merchant capital and bound into urban commercial networks. Second, Schley insists on the role of deliberate institutional action — charters, public investment, boosterism — in deciding which cities won. Geography mattered, but so did politics: the Erie Canal was a policy choice, and so was every railroad charter that followed it. For a course about urban institutions, Schley's essay establishes the foundational point that the American urban system was built, not merely grown.

Key Concept: The Walking City

The pre-industrial American city, typically less than two miles across, in which nearly all movement was on foot. Land uses and social classes mixed at close quarters, and the waterfront — the point of commercial exchange — was the center of wealth and power. Its dissolution, beginning with horse-drawn omnibuses and streetcars in the 1830s–1850s, launched the sorting of American urban space by class, race, and function that later chapters will trace.

Industrial Takeoff: The Manufacturing Belt

By the Civil War era, the balance was tipping. Steam power freed factories from riverside waterpower sites and let them locate in cities, near labor and rail connections. Coal — Pennsylvania anthracite, then bituminous coal from the Appalachian fields — became the energy base of an emerging industrial economy. In the decades after 1870, a distinct manufacturing belt crystallized: a band of industrial cities stretching from New England and the mid-Atlantic across upstate New York, Pennsylvania, Ohio, Michigan, Indiana, and Illinois. Within it, cities specialized. Pittsburgh, sitting on coking coal at the head of the Ohio River, became the nation's forge: Andrew Carnegie's steelworks pioneered the integration of blast furnaces and Bessemer converters into continuous, high-volume production. Cleveland, midway between Lake Superior iron ore and Appalachian coal, built steel, machinery, and — under John D. Rockefeller — the Standard Oil refining empire. Chicago became the supreme example of the industrial metropolis: railroad hub, grain market, lumber market, and home of the Union Stock Yards (opened 1865), whose "disassembly lines" for processing livestock prefigured the moving assembly line. Chicago passed one million residents by 1890 and reached 1,698,575 in 1900 — a city that had multiplied several hundredfold in sixty years.

Industrial organization changed as dramatically as industrial geography. The late nineteenth century was the age of the giant corporation. Firms like Carnegie Steel and Standard Oil pursued vertical integration — controlling every stage of production from raw material to finished product — while merger waves produced horizontal consolidation on an unprecedented scale. The climax came in 1901, when J. P. Morgan merged Carnegie Steel with its major rivals to create United States Steel, capitalized at $1.4 billion: the world's first billion-dollar corporation. These corporate behemoths were urban institutions through and through, headquartered in downtown office buildings, dependent on city labor markets, and increasingly powerful actors in city politics.

The Metropolis, 1880–1929

Between 1880 and 1929, American urbanization and industrialization accelerated together to a pace never seen before or since. The urban share of the national population rose from 28.2 percent in 1880 to 39.6 percent in 1900 — and then, in 1920, the census recorded a threshold moment: for the first time, a majority of Americans, 51.2 percent, lived in urban places. The rural republic of Jefferson's imagination was statistically dead.

The people powering this growth came from farther afield than ever. Between the 1880s and the outbreak of World War I, immigration shifted toward southern and eastern Europe — Italians, Poles, Russian Jews, Greeks, Slavs — and the newcomers concentrated overwhelmingly in industrial cities, filling the mills, packinghouses, and garment lofts. When the war cut off European immigration in 1914, northern factories turned to a new labor source: Black southerners. The Great Migration was under way, with an estimated 400,000 African Americans leaving the South between 1916 and 1918 alone, and roughly 1.6 million in the first wave through 1940, bound for Chicago, Detroit, New York, Philadelphia, Pittsburgh, and Cleveland. Their arrival — and the ferocious white resistance it met in housing and labor markets — set in motion institutional dynamics of segregation that will occupy us for much of this course.

The physical city was transformed as well. Electric streetcars (after 1888) stretched cities outward; the skyscraper stacked them upward. Chicago's Home Insurance Building (1885), designed by William Le Baron Jenney with a load-bearing metal frame, is conventionally counted the first skyscraper at ten stories; within a generation, steel framing and the electric elevator had produced the towering downtown skylines of Chicago and New York, culminating in the Woolworth Building of our opening vignette. The skyscraper was more than engineering: it was the corporation made visible, a monument to the concentration of administrative work — and of clerical employment, increasingly female — in the central business district.

Reading: Jonathan Rees, "Industrialization and Urbanization in the United States, 1880–1929"

The second assigned reading, by Jonathan Rees, a historian of American industry and labor at Colorado State University–Pueblo, covers this second period for the same Oxford encyclopedia project. Rees defines his terms carefully — industrialization as factory production using machinery and a divided labor force, urbanization as the growth of cities in population and territory — and argues that between 1880 and 1929 both expanded faster than ever before, with industrialization acting as the primary driver of urban growth.

What changed after 1880, in Rees's account, was the character of industrialization itself. Earlier factories had raised output chiefly through the division of labor: breaking work into small, repetitive tasks assigned to particular workers. After 1880, the emphasis shifted to mechanization — the outright replacement of people with machines — made possible by new technologies, above all the electrical grid, which began developing in the early 1880s and freed factory design from the shafts and belts of steam power. Henry Ford's moving assembly line, introduced at Highland Park in 1913, fused division of labor and mechanization into mass production. The cumulative result was extraordinary. As Rees notes:

The total manufacturing output of the United States was twenty-eight times greater in 1929 than it was in 1859.

— Jonathan Rees, Industrialization and Urbanization in the United States, 1880–1929 (Oxford Research Encyclopedia of American History, 2016)

Rees's essay matters for this course because it links production to consumption and to urban form. Mass production required mass distribution and mass consumption, and cities supplied both: dense markets of wage-earning consumers, connected by rail to national networks of goods. The same era that built the blast furnace also built the department store, the mail-order catalog, the chain store, and the advertising industry — institutions that turned industrial output into a new way of urban life. That new way of life is the subject of our third reading.

From Counting-House to Selling Floor: The Institutions of Consumption

Consider the department store, arguably the signature commercial institution of the industrial metropolis. Its American pioneers included A. T. Stewart in New York and, most influentially, John Wanamaker, who in 1876 opened his "Grand Depot" in a converted Philadelphia railroad freight building. Wanamaker's innovations — clearly marked fixed prices in place of haggling, money-back guarantees, lavish newspaper advertising, and amenities like electric lighting (1878) and in-store restaurants — redefined shopping as an experience rather than a transaction. In Chicago, Marshall Field built a retail palace on State Street around the credo "give the lady what she wants." The department store created new downtown geographies, new forms of respectable public life for middle-class women (as shoppers, and as a vast new female sales workforce), and a new civic spectacle of plate glass, display windows, and seasonal pageantry. It was, in short, a machine for teaching urban Americans how to be consumers.

Reading: Warren Susman, "Culture as History" — Character, Personality, and the Consumer Self

Our third reading shifts register from economic to cultural history. Warren Susman (1927–1985), a legendary teacher at Rutgers University, collected his most influential essays in Culture as History: The Transformation of American Society in the Twentieth Century (1984). Susman's overarching argument is that the material transformation we have been tracing — the passage, in the formulations he catalogs, from a producer to a consumer society, from scarcity to abundance — was simultaneously a cultural transformation, and that historians must read culture (advice manuals, films, advertising, popular language) as evidence of how ordinary people experienced and made sense of a changing social order. He pointed to the economist Simon Patten, whose The New Basis of Civilization (1907) argued that a society moving from scarcity toward abundance would require a new kind of self.

Susman's most famous essay, "'Personality' and the Making of Twentieth-Century Culture," makes that argument concrete through a close reading of self-improvement manuals. Nineteenth-century America, he argues, was a "culture of character." Surveying the era's advice literature, he found character linked again and again to a distinctive vocabulary: citizenship, duty, democracy, work, golden deeds, honor, reputation, morals, integrity, and "above all, manhood" — with Emerson's definition of character, "moral order through the medium of individual nature," the most popular quotation of all. Character was a producer's ideal: self-control, self-sacrifice, and moral seriousness suited to a world of scarcity, hard work, and small communities where reputations were earned over a lifetime.

Around the turn of the twentieth century — Susman locates the pivot in the first decade of the 1900s, with the new advice manuals clustering between 1900 and 1920 — a rival ideal emerged: personality. The new manuals answered a distinctly urban question: in a mass society of strangers, crowds, and quick impressions, how does one stand out? Their recurring definition, Susman reports, was that "personality is the quality of being Somebody." The adjectives attached to personality tell the story of the shift: fascinating, stunning, attractive, magnetic, glowing, masterful, creative, dominant, forceful. Where character asked to be respected, personality asked to be noticed. As Susman put it in the essay's most quoted lines:

The social role demanded of all in the new culture of personality was that of a performer. Every American was to become a performing self.

— Warren Susman, Culture as History: The Transformation of American Society in the Twentieth Century (1984)

Susman emphasizes that "the vision of self-sacrifice began to yield to that of self-realization," and he notes the irony that manuals urging readers to be unique offered them identical, standardized instructions for achieving distinction. The new self was fitted to new urban institutions: the department store and its display windows, advertising and its promises, the corporate office where advancement depended on impressing superiors, and above all the movies — the star system was born around 1910 — which made the performing self a national model. For urban historians, Susman's significance is methodological as much as substantive: he shows that the city's institutional revolution reached all the way down into the vocabulary Americans used to describe who they were. Urbanization and industrialization did not just relocate Americans; they re-made them.

Key Concept: Producer Culture vs. Consumer Culture

Shorthand for the cultural shift accompanying industrial abundance. A producer culture prizes work, thrift, self-denial, and moral "character" — values suited to scarcity. A consumer culture, emerging with mass production, mass retailing, advertising, and mass entertainment between roughly 1880 and 1930, prizes leisure, spending, self-expression, and "personality." Susman's essays in Culture as History remain the classic statement of this transition.

The Arc in Sum

Set the three readings side by side and a single arc emerges. Schley shows commercial cities and deliberate public investment creating the conditions for industrial growth before 1870. Rees shows industry, supercharged by mechanization and electricity after 1880, driving cities to unprecedented size and pulling the nation past the urban-majority threshold of 1920. Susman shows the cultural consequence: a new consumer society demanding a new kind of self. The prosperity of the 1920s — the decade of Ford's Model T, the radio, the movie palace, and the skyline — seemed to confirm the arc's upward direction. Then, in October 1929, the stock market crashed, and the industrial metropolis entered its greatest crisis. That crisis, and the institutional responses it provoked, begins our next chapter.

Broad Arcs II: Immigration, Migration, and Race in the American City

How successive waves of newcomers—European immigrants, excluded Asians, and Black southerners—built, contested, and were sorted within the American city between the 1840s and the 1960s.

Learning Objectives

  • Explain what Friedrich Engels saw in the industrial "shock city" of the 1840s and why his account of Manchester and London became a template for urban social criticism.
  • Trace the major waves of immigration to American cities from 1840 to 1924, and the nativist and legal reactions—from the Know-Nothings to the Chinese Exclusion Act and the Johnson-Reed quotas—that they provoked.
  • Describe how the quota era (1924–1965) hollowed out urban ethnic enclaves and how the Hart-Celler Act of 1965 remade immigrant cities.
  • Analyze the causes, geography, and consequences of the Great Migrations of Black southerners (1914–1970), including racial violence, restrictive covenants, and the flowering of Black urban culture.

A Walk Through the Shock City

In the winter of 1842, a twenty-two-year-old German arrived in Manchester, England, to take up a clerkship in his father's cotton-thread firm, Ermen & Engels. His family hoped that a stint in the countinghouse would cure the young man of his radical enthusiasms. It did the opposite. For nearly two years Friedrich Engels spent his evenings walking the working-class districts of the world's first industrial metropolis—often guided by Mary Burns, a young Irish factory worker—recording what the factory system did to the people who served it. The book he published in 1845, The Condition of the Working-Class in England, gave the world its first great anatomy of the industrial city.

Why open a chapter on American urban history in Manchester? Because Manchester was what the historian Asa Briggs later called the "shock city" of the age—the place contemporaries visited to glimpse the urban future, in the way later generations would visit Chicago or Los Angeles. The forces Engels documented—mass in-migration (much of it Irish), industrial labor demand, residential sorting by class, and the mutual invisibility of rich and poor—would replay themselves in New York, Boston, Philadelphia, and Chicago over the following century. And the populations were literally connected: the same Irish famine refugees who crowded Manchester's cellars in the 1840s also crowded the wards of Boston and New York. This chapter follows two intertwined arcs across American urban history: the century of mass immigration and its restriction (roughly 1840–1965), and the Great Migrations of African Americans out of the rural South (1914–1970). Both arcs remade the institutions of the American city; both provoked ferocious campaigns of exclusion; and both raise the chapter's central analytical question—what, exactly, is an "ethnic neighborhood," and who decides its boundaries?

Engels and the Great Towns

Chapter 4 of Engels's book, "The Great Towns," opens not in Manchester but in London, with one of the most famous passages in the literature of the city. Engels concedes the grandeur of the Thames and the docks—"a man cannot collect himself, but is lost in the marvel of England's greatness"—and then turns the image inside out. The metropolis, he argues, purchases its splendor with the atomization of its people:

The hundreds of thousands of all classes and ranks crowding past each other, are they not all human beings with the same qualities and powers, and with the same interest in being happy? … And still they crowd by one another as though they had nothing in common, nothing to do with one another, and their only agreement is the tacit one, that each keep to his own side of the pavement, so as not to delay the opposing streams of the crowd, while it occurs to no man to honour another with so much as a glance. The brutal indifference, the unfeeling isolation of each in his private interest, becomes the more repellent and offensive, the more these individuals are crowded together, within a limited space. … The dissolution of mankind into monads, of which each one has a separate principle, the world of atoms, is here carried out to its utmost extreme. Hence it comes, too, that the social war, the war of each against all, is here openly declared.

— Friedrich Engels, The Condition of the Working-Class in England in 1844, "The Great Towns" (1845)

Notice the argumentative move. The crowd's indifference is not, for Engels, a personal moral failing of Londoners; it is the visible expression of a competitive social order—"the war of each against all"—in which capital is the weapon and the poor absorb every disadvantage. What is true of London, he insists, "is true of Manchester, Birmingham, Leeds, is true of all great towns." The city does not create inequality, but it concentrates and reveals it.

The heart of the chapter is Engels's block-by-block tour of Manchester, conducted with the precision of a surveyor and the fury of a prosecutor. In the Old Town along the river Irk, he found courts reachable only through covered passages, containing "unqualifiedly the most horrible dwellings which I have yet beheld." His description of the river itself became a set piece of Victorian social exposure:

At the bottom flows, or rather stagnates, the Irk, a narrow, coal-black, foul-smelling stream, full of debris and refuse, which it deposits on the shallower right bank. In dry weather, a long string of the most disgusting, blackish-green, slime pools are left standing on this bank, from the depths of which bubbles of miasmatic gas constantly arise and give forth a stench unendurable even on the bridge forty or fifty feet above the surface of the stream.

— Friedrich Engels, The Condition of the Working-Class in England in 1844, "The Great Towns" (1845)

For urban historians, though, the chapter's most enduring insight is spatial. Engels observed that Manchester was "peculiarly built, so that a person may live in it for years, and go in and out daily without coming into contact with a working-people's quarter or even with workers." The main thoroughfares out of the Exchange were lined with shops that screened the misery behind them; he had "never seen so systematic a shutting out of the working-class from the thoroughfares, so tender a concealment of everything which might affront the eye and the nerves of the bourgeoisie, as in Manchester." This was segregation by class, produced—Engels shrewdly noted—by "unconscious tacit agreement, as well as with outspoken conscious determination," rather than by any statute. Hold onto that formulation. When we reach the American "Black Belt" later in this chapter, we will meet a segregation produced by exactly this combination of market habit and deliberate design—only organized around race rather than class, and enforced far more rigidly.

Key Concept: The Shock City

A "shock city" (Asa Briggs's term) is the urban place that a generation treats as the exhibit of its own future—visited, studied, and argued over as the extreme case of contemporary change. Manchester played this role in the 1840s; Chicago played it for Americans in the 1890s; postwar Los Angeles arguably played it in the twentieth century. The concept reminds us that cities are not just places but arguments: observers project their hopes and anxieties onto them.

The First Wave: Building the Immigrant City, 1840–1924

Hidetaka Hirota's encyclopedia article "Immigration to American Cities, 1800–1924" surveys the century in which the United States absorbed, by his count, nearly thirty-six million immigrants between 1820 and 1924. The composition shifted over time: before the Civil War the vast majority came from northern and western Europe (with Chinese immigration to the West Coast beginning in the late 1840s); at the turn of the twentieth century the major sending regions shifted to southern and eastern Europe, with Asians and Mexicans forming a growing share. Hirota's central claim is double-edged: immigrants "contributed to the social, cultural, political, economic, and physical growth" of American cities and supplied the labor for industrialization—yet those same cities incubated an "intense nativism" that ultimately produced state and federal laws to keep out foreigners deemed undesirable: the poor, southern and eastern Europeans, and Asians. Cities, in short, were where the American immigration experience—and the American exclusion experience—was made.

The mid-century rupture was the Irish famine. Between 1845 and the early 1850s, the potato blight drove roughly a million and a half Irish to the United States, most of them Catholic and many destitute. They concentrated overwhelmingly in port cities: by the mid-1850s more than a quarter of New York City's population was Irish-born, and Boston—a compact Puritan-descended town—found itself transformed within a decade. German immigrants arrived in comparable numbers, fanning out to inland cities such as Cincinnati, St. Louis, and Milwaukee, where German-language churches, newspapers, beer gardens, and Turnvereine created the first large-scale ethnic institutional worlds in urban America.

The reaction was swift. The 1850s saw the spectacular rise of the Know-Nothing movement—formally the American Party, grown out of nativist secret societies—which fused anti-Catholicism, temperance, and anxieties about pauperism into the most successful third-party surge of the century, capturing the Massachusetts legislature and dozens of congressional seats in 1854–1855. In his own scholarship (notably Expelling the Poor, 2017), Hirota has shown that nativism was not merely rhetorical: state officials in Massachusetts and New York deported thousands of poor Irish immigrants back across the Atlantic decades before the federal government asserted control over immigration—an institutional prehistory of American deportation policy that began at the state and municipal level.

Reception, too, was institutionalized in the city. In 1855 New York State opened Castle Garden at the tip of Manhattan, the nation's first formal immigrant landing depot, which processed roughly eight million arrivals before the federal government assumed control and opened Ellis Island in 1892. Some twelve million immigrants passed through Ellis Island before it closed in 1954—Italians, eastern European Jews, Poles, Greeks, Slavs—the "new immigrants" who filled the tenement districts, garment lofts, steel mills, and packinghouses of industrial urban America. Their neighborhoods—the Lower East Side, Little Italies, Polish "Polonias"—became simultaneously objects of settlement-house reform, Progressive-era social science, and nativist alarm.

Gatekeeping and Exclusion

The legal architecture of exclusion was built piece by piece, and it was built first against Asians. The Page Act of 1875 barred Chinese women suspected of prostitution—in practice, most Chinese women. The Chinese Exclusion Act of 1882 then suspended the immigration of Chinese laborers outright: the first federal law to ban a group by race and class, renewed in 1892, made permanent in 1902, and not repealed until 1943. On the West Coast the counterpart to Ellis Island was Angel Island in San Francisco Bay (1910–1940)—less a welcome station than a detention and interrogation facility, where Chinese arrivals claiming citizenship as "paper sons" were held for weeks or months and where detainees carved poems of frustration into the barracks walls. The contrast between the two islands captures the era's essential asymmetry: for Europeans, gatekeeping was a filter; for Asians, it was a wall.

Restriction then widened to Europeans. A literacy test for immigrants passed over presidential veto in 1917 (along with an "Asiatic Barred Zone"), and after the First World War, restrictionists—armed with eugenicist race science and wartime "100 percent Americanism"—won the decisive victory. The Emergency Quota Act of 1921 and then the Johnson-Reed Immigration Act of 1924 capped annual European immigration with national-origins quotas deliberately rigged against southern and eastern Europeans: the 1924 law set each nationality's quota at 2 percent of its share of the U.S. population in the 1890 census—before the "new immigration" had peaked—and barred entirely all "aliens ineligible to citizenship," a formula that ended Japanese immigration. Total quota immigration was ultimately squeezed to about 150,000 per year. Significantly, the Western Hemisphere was left outside the quotas, keeping the door open for Mexican and Canadian labor migration. As Charlotte Brooks observes, the 1924 act was "in large part the result of a deep political and cultural divide in America between heavily immigrant cities and far less diverse small towns and rural areas"—it was, among other things, a law passed by rural and small-town America against the immigrant metropolis.

Key Concept: Chain Migration and the Enclave

Most migrants did not choose destinations from a map; they followed kin and townspeople who could offer a bed, a job lead, and news from home. This "chain migration" explains why particular blocks filled with people from particular villages—and why ethnic neighborhoods functioned less as walled ghettos than as switchboards connecting old-world origins to new-world labor markets. The same mechanism, operating through letters, Black newspapers, and railroad lines, later channeled the Great Migration to specific northern cities.

The Quota Era and After, 1924–1965

Charlotte Brooks's companion article, "Immigration to American Cities, 1925–2017," picks up the story at the moment the gates closed. Her argument is that restriction, in combination with mid-century urban policy, transformed the interior geography of the immigrant city: the 1924 law, "together with growing residential segregation, midcentury federal urban policy, and postwar suburbanization, undermined scores of ethnic enclaves in American cities between 1925 and the 1960s." With replenishment from abroad cut off, the tenement districts aged and emptied; the children and grandchildren of the "new immigrants" Americanized, intermarried across European lines, and—crucially—gained access to the suburban home-ownership escalator that federal mortgage insurance built after 1934, an escalator largely closed to nonwhites (a story we take up in Chapter 6).

Brooks emphasizes that the quota era was punctuated by episodes of coerced removal that reshaped cities as surely as any arrival: the deportation and "repatriation" of Mexicans—and many of their U.S.-citizen children—during the Great Depression, and the wartime incarceration of West Coast Japanese Americans in 1942, which emptied urban Japantowns overnight (some, like San Francisco's, were partly repopulated during the war by Black migrant war workers). Meanwhile the wartime and postwar shift of jobs toward suburbs and the Sunbelt drained the older immigrant industrial districts of their economic reason for being.

The hinge of the modern era is the Immigration and Nationality Act of 1965—the Hart-Celler Act—which abolished the national-origins quotas and substituted a preference system built around family reunification and skills. Its authors expected modest change; instead it opened sustained mass immigration from Asia, Latin America, and eventually Africa. For cities the consequences were profound: in Brooks's assessment, post-1965 immigration "helped to revitalize many depressed urban areas and inner-ring suburbs," repopulating neighborhoods the urban crisis had emptied and generating new commercial corridors from Flushing to Koreatown to Little Havana. Her closing observation connects past to present: responses to newcomers have ranged "from welcoming to hostile," but urban areas with long histories of immigrant incorporation remain the places most politically supportive of immigrants—"just as they were a century ago." The city-versus-hinterland divide of 1924, in other words, is still with us.

The Great Migration and Black Urban Life

The second great arc of this chapter is internal. Tyina Steptoe's article "The Great Migrations and Black Urban Life in the United States, 1914–1970" describes the demographic revolution by which Black America became urban. In 1900 roughly nine in ten African Americans lived in the South, most in rural, agricultural settings. Across two waves of migration—approximately 1914–1930 and 1941–1970—some six million Black southerners moved to cities: to the urban South itself (Houston, Atlanta, Memphis), and to the Northeast, Midwest, and West Coast. Note Steptoe's plural—Migrations—and her inclusion of southern cities: the movement was not a single South-to-North exodus but a sustained, multidirectional urbanization.

The causes were both push and pull. The push: the Jim Crow regime of disenfranchisement, segregation, lynching, and debt-bound sharecropping, compounded after 1910 by the boll weevil's destruction of the cotton economy and by catastrophic floods. The pull: the First World War, which simultaneously cut off European immigration and exploded northern industrial labor demand, opening jobs in steel, meatpacking, and railroads that had previously been reserved for immigrant workers. Information traveled through institutions: labor agents, railroad lines, letters home, and above all the Black press. Robert Abbott's Chicago Defender—circulated through the South by Pullman porters and banned in some southern towns—urged readers northward with campaigns like the "Great Northern Drive" of 1917, printing train schedules, job notices, and unsparing accounts of southern violence. Chicago's Black population leapt from about 44,000 in 1910 to 109,000 in 1920 and 234,000 by 1930 (Figure 3.3).

Arrival did not mean welcome. Migrants were channeled into narrow districts—Chicago's South Side "Black Belt," New York's Harlem—by a mesh of discriminatory institutions. After the Supreme Court struck down explicit racial zoning ordinances in Buchanan v. Warley (1917), white property owners, realtors, and neighborhood "improvement associations" turned to racially restrictive covenants: private deed clauses barring sale or rental to Black occupants (and often Asians and Jews), which blanketed whole sections of northern cities and remained judicially enforceable until Shelley v. Kraemer (1948). Where legal instruments failed, violence served: bombings of Black homes on the borders of the Black Belt were routine in Chicago between 1917 and 1921. The "Red Summer" of 1919 brought antiblack mob violence to dozens of cities; in Chicago that July, the drowning of the Black teenager Eugene Williams—stoned after his raft drifted across the invisible color line at a Lake Michigan beach—ignited a week of rioting that left 38 dead (23 of them Black), more than 500 injured, and roughly a thousand families burned out of their homes.

Yet the story Steptoe tells is emphatically not one of victimization alone. Within the constrained space of the segregated city, migrants built dense institutional worlds—churches, lodges, banks, insurance companies, newspapers, political machines—and a self-conscious new culture. Migrants "often referred to themselves as 'New Negroes,'" she writes, "pointing to their social, political, and cultural achievements, as well as their use of armed self-defense during violent racial confrontations, as evidence of their new stance on race." Harlem in the 1920s became the capital of this New Negro movement: the Harlem Renaissance of Alain Locke, Langston Hughes, and Zora Neale Hurston, alongside Marcus Garvey's mass movement; Chicago's South Side incubated urban blues, gospel, and jazz. Northern ballots translated numbers into power—Chicago's Oscar De Priest became in 1928 the first African American elected to Congress in the twentieth century. The Great Migration, Steptoe shows, made Black Americans an urban people and made the "race question" a national, urban question—setting the stage for both the civil rights insurgencies and the urban crises examined later in this book.

Key Concept: Restrictive Covenants

A restrictive covenant is a clause written into a property deed binding present and future owners—here, forbidding sale or occupancy by specified racial groups. Because covenants were "private" contracts, they evaded the constitutional ban on racial zoning for three decades, illustrating a recurring theme of this course: when one institution of exclusion is dismantled, its work often migrates into another legal form. Shelley v. Kraemer (1948) made covenants judicially unenforceable, but their spatial legacy—the hyper-segregated "Black Belt"—endured through redlining, blockbusting, and public-housing siting.

Ethnicity and the Neighborhood: Ghetto or Switchboard?

How should we conceptualize the neighborhoods all these migrations produced? Jordan Stanger-Ross's article "Ethnicity and US Neighborhoods" treats ethnicity not as a fixed inheritance but as a set of "social, cultural, and political processes whereby immigrants and their children cease to be 'foreign' and yet retain practices and networks" connecting them to places of origin. Neighborhoods were central to those processes: "magnets for new arrivals," they "both emerged from and reinforced connections among people of common origins," from the German districts of the mid-nineteenth century to the Jewish Lower East Side and San Francisco's Chinatown, through the mid-century emptying-out of white ethnic districts, to the new enclaves that formed after 1965 in a suburbanized metropolis.

The classic model of the ethnic neighborhood came from the Chicago School of sociology in the 1920s. Robert Park and Ernest Burgess mapped the city as concentric zones through which immigrant groups passed in an orderly "race relations cycle": arrival in the slum, then assimilation and outward movement. Louis Wirth's The Ghetto (1928) canonized the term for the transitional immigrant quarter itself. The model was elegant, hugely influential—and, later scholars showed, misleading. Revisionist historians demonstrated that European ethnic "ghettos" were never homogeneous: in early twentieth-century Chicago, no European group ever constituted anything close to an exclusive majority of "its" named district—Little Italy, Polonia, and the old Jewish West Side were all mixed neighborhoods, symbols more than containments—whereas the Black Belt really was nearly all-Black, and its residents, unlike European immigrants, could not leave when they prospered. Thomas Philpott's The Slum and the Ghetto (1978) made the point statistically: the immigrant "ghetto" was a way station; the Black ghetto was a cage. Stanger-Ross's own comparative work (Staying Italian, 2009) adds a further twist: ethnic persistence was not simply the residue of failed assimilation but could be an active, adaptive strategy, taking different institutional forms—parish, real estate market, workplace, marriage network—in different cities.

This scholarly debate matters far beyond the seminar room. If policymakers imagine all ethnic concentration on the Chicago School model—a temporary stop on a universal escalator—then Black segregation appears as a cultural failure to assimilate. If, instead, we distinguish voluntary clustering sustained by chain migration from involuntary confinement enforced by covenant, violence, and credit discrimination, the "ghetto" ceases to be one thing, and the policy questions change accordingly. Engels, remember, saw Manchester's class segregation as the product of "unconscious tacit agreement" joined to "outspoken conscious determination." A century of American urban history proved how much more durable that combination becomes when organized around race.

Urban Politics: Bosses, Machines, and the Business of Government

How machines like Tammany Hall converted votes into jobs, favors, and fortunes—and why, as Merton argued, the "corrupt" system endured by doing work no other urban institution would do.

Learning Objectives

  • Describe how machines were organized—precinct captain, district leader, boss—and how they held coalitions together through personal service and patronage.
  • Explain and evaluate Plunkitt's distinction between "honest graft" and "dishonest graft."
  • Apply Merton's concepts of manifest and latent functions to explain why machines persisted despite decades of exposure and reform.
  • Situate machine politics within Connolly's account of urban political development before 1940.
  • Trace public employment as a labor question, from the spoils system through civil service reform to the Boston police strike of 1919.

The Philosopher of the Bootblack Stand

Around the turn of the twentieth century, anyone who wanted an audience with one of the most powerful politicians in New York City needed no appointment. George Washington Plunkitt—leader of the Fifteenth Assembly District, Sachem of the Tammany Society, former State Senator, and a self-made millionaire—kept no office. His headquarters was the bootblack stand in the New York County Courthouse, where he received constituents, transacted business, and held forth on "very practical politics." The journalist William L. Riordon wrote down these courthouse sermons and collected them in 1905 as Plunkitt of Tammany Hall: a machine politician explaining, with candor and evident pride, exactly how the system worked.

The setting was an irony Plunkitt never acknowledged: the courthouse where he polished his epigrams was the infamous "Tweed Courthouse," whose wildly padded construction contracts had helped bring down Tammany boss William M. Tweed a generation earlier. Tweed died in jail in 1878; Plunkitt died rich and respected in 1924. The difference between those fates is, in a sense, this chapter's subject. Plunkitt insisted there was a bright line between the plunder that destroyed Tweed and the "honest graft" that enriched men like himself—and that the machine was not a parasite on the city but the thing that actually governed it. Reformers called this sophistry. But in 1949 the sociologist Robert K. Merton argued, in more clinical language, that Plunkitt had a point: the machine survived every scandal because it performed social functions that respectable institutions were failing to perform. Both the machine's operations and its functions are the keys to how American cities were run between the 1840s and the New Deal.

The Rise of the Machine

The machine was the creature of a particular moment: the collision of mass democracy with explosive urban growth. By the 1840s, property qualifications for voting had largely fallen for white men, just as famine-driven Irish immigration poured tens of thousands of poor newcomers into New York, Boston, and Philadelphia each year. Cities suddenly contained enormous blocs of voters who owned nothing, knew no officials, and needed everything—jobs, housing, protection, naturalization papers. Formal government, fragmented among mayors, councils, state legislatures, and independent boards, was poorly equipped to serve them. Party organizations stepped into the gap, trading concrete help for reliable votes.

The historian James J. Connolly, surveying urban politics before 1940, frames the story this way: the convergence of mass politics and urban growth in the nineteenth century "produced sharp debates over the character of politics in urban settings." What contemporaries came to call machine politics—concentrated in the industrial East and Midwest—drew fierce criticism for its reliance on patronage and favor trading, its intense partisanship, and the plebeian origins of the "bosses" who practiced it. That last complaint deserves emphasis: much of the outrage at boss rule was class outrage that saloonkeepers, ex-butchers' boys, and immigrants' sons were running great cities. Opposition began among upper- and middle-class businessmen; by the Progressive Era it had been joined by labor activists, women reformers, and even some ethnic spokespersons. Connolly cautions against taking the melodrama of "boss rule" at face value: "the machine" was partly a polemical label reformers pinned on opponents, and real party organizations were looser and more contested than the image of the all-powerful boss suggests.

Tammany Hall, the Democratic organization of Manhattan, was the archetype. Founded in 1789 as a fraternal society, it evolved by mid-century into the country's most sophisticated vote-gathering operation, cultivating the immigrant voters—above all the Irish—whom nativists scorned. Tweed's ring looted the treasury on a heroic scale in the late 1860s through padded bills and kickbacks, until the New York Times and the cartoonist Thomas Nast brought it down in 1871. Tweed's successors learned the lesson: "Honest John" Kelly, Richard Croker, and Charles Francis Murphy ran a disciplined organization that dominated New York politics, with interruptions, into the 1920s. Murphy—whose foreword to Riordon's book praises Plunkitt as "a straight organization man"—would eventually sponsor Al Smith and Robert F. Wagner, channeling machine politics toward social legislation. Similar organizations flourished in Philadelphia, Memphis, Jersey City, and Kansas City.

Key Concept: The Political Machine

A hierarchical party organization—boss at the top, district leaders in the middle, precinct captains at the base—that won elections by trading material benefits (jobs, relief, favors, protection) for votes, financing itself through patronage, contracts, and contributions from interests that needed things from city government. Plunkitt described Tammany as "a great big machine, with every part adjusted delicate to do its own particular work." The word "machine" was an insult; its targets preferred "the organization."

How the Machine Worked: Study Human Nature and Act Accordin'

Plunkitt's talks are the best available user's manual for this system, and the essential one is "To Hold Your District: Study Human Nature and Act Accordin'." His first principle was contempt for theory: "You can't study human nature in books," and a college education was, if anything, a handicap. What mattered was personal knowledge: "I know every man, woman, and child in the Fifteenth District, except them that's been born this summer—and I know some of them, too." He recruited young men not with arguments but with attention—the neighborhood singer joined the Washington Hall Glee Club, the sandlot star the baseball club: "I rope them all in by givin' them opportunities to show themselves off. I don't trouble them with political arguments."

For poor families, the machine offered what charity did not: immediate, unconditional help from a neighbor rather than an investigator. Plunkitt's fire relief is the classic statement of machine welfare:

If a family is burned out I don't ask whether they are Republicans or Democrats, and I don't refer them to the Charity Organization Society, which would investigate their case in a month or two and decide they were worthy of help about the time they are dead from starvation. I just get quarters for them, buy clothes for them if their clothes were burned up, and fix them up till they get things runnin' again. It's philanthropy, but it's politics, too—mighty good politics. Who can tell how many votes one of these fires bring me? The poor are the most grateful people in the world.

— William L. Riordon, Plunkitt of Tammany Hall (1905)

Riordon's closing chapter, "Strenuous Life of the Tammany District Leader," reconstructs a single day from Plunkitt's diary, and it reads like the schedule of a one-man social service agency: roused at 2 a.m. to bail out a saloonkeeper; at a tenement fire by 6 a.m.; in police court at 8:30 securing the discharge of four "drunks"; paying a poor family's rent to stop a dispossession; finding jobs for four unemployed men; attending an Italian funeral and a Jewish one; buying ice cream at a church fair; in bed at midnight. His headquarters, Riordon noted, "bears the inscription, 'Never closed.'" A philanthropist? "Not at all... He is playing politics all the time." Rival leaders raced to funerals and stationed spies at the City Hall marriage bureau so their wedding presents would arrive first. The machine's genius was this fusion of sentiment and calculation—every kindness was real, and every kindness was booked.

Holding the organization together required patronage. "Men ain't in politics for nothin'," Plunkitt observed. "They want to get somethin' out of it." When Tammany lost City Hall, he placed "several hundred" followers in private railroad jobs, and swapped favors across party lines: "Me and the Republicans are enemies just one day in the year—election day... The rest of the time it's live and let live with us." Partisanship was a business rivalry among professionals who agreed that political work should pay.

Honest Graft: "I Seen My Opportunities and I Took 'Em"

What did the leaders get out of it? Plunkitt's answer, in the book's most famous talk, entered the American political vocabulary:

Everybody is talkin' these days about Tammany men growin' rich on graft, but nobody thinks of drawin' the distinction between honest graft and dishonest graft... I've made a big fortune out of the game, and I'm gettin' richer every day, but I've not gone in for dishonest graft—blackmailin' gamblers, saloonkeepers, disorderly people, etc.... There's an honest graft, and I'm an example of how it works. I might sum up the whole thing by sayin': "I seen my opportunities and I took 'em."

— William L. Riordon, Plunkitt of Tammany Hall (1905)

Honest graft, in Plunkitt's telling, was profiting from inside information rather than stealing from the treasury. Tipped off that the city planned a new park or bridge, he bought the land in advance and sold it back "at my own price"—including, in one cherished anecdote, a swamp the park could not be completed without. "Ain't it perfectly honest to charge a good price and make a profit on my investment and foresight?" he asked. "It's just like lookin' ahead in Wall Street or in the coffee or cotton market." He rigged a city auction by buying off rival bidders, winning 250,000 granite paving blocks for $2.50. The books always balanced; reform administrations hunting for "public robberies" never found any, because Tammany's department heads had merely "looked after their friends, within the law." He proposed his own epitaph: "George W. Plunkitt. He Seen His Opportunities, and He Took 'Em."

The distinction did real rhetorical work. In "On The Shame of the Cities," Plunkitt takes on the muckraker Lincoln Steffens directly: Steffens "means well but, like all reformers, he don't know how to make distinctions" between "political looters and politicians who make a fortune out of politics by keepin' their eyes wide open." The Philadelphia Republican gang robbed the bank and stayed "to pick up the nickels and pennies"—one almshouse superintendent, Plunkitt marveled, stole the zinc roof off the building—while Tammany "never monkeyed with the penal code." The big city was a political Garden of Eden, an orchard with a single tree marked "Penal Code Tree—Poison"; only fools ate from it. Notice what the distinction conceals as well as reveals. "Honest graft" was still the conversion of public trust into private fortune: the profits on Plunkitt's swamp were paid by taxpayers, on knowledge that belonged, in any fiduciary sense, to the public. But Plunkitt was making a claim reformers had to answer: that his self-dealing was continuous with ordinary American capitalism, and that the moral line muckrakers drew around politics was one they declined to draw around business.

Plunkitt reserved his deepest scorn for the reformers. In "Reformers Only Mornin' Glories," he ran through the roll of defunct reform committees and pronounced his verdict: "They were mornin' glories—looked lovely in the mornin' and withered up in a short time, while the regular machines went on flourishin' forever, like fine old oaks." His explanation was professionalism: "Politics is as much a regular business as the grocery or the dry-goods or the drug business. You've got to be trained up to it or you're sure to fail." The amateur reformer in politics was like Plunkitt going up to Columbia to teach Greek. In "Tammany Leaders Not Bookworms" he elaborated: each leader was fitted to his district—"We don't put a silk stockin' on the Bowery"—while he himself, a "cosmopolitan," could "talk grammar" with the silk stockings and drop "all monkeyshines" among the poor. And "Bosses Preserve the Nation" offered the machine's grandest self-justification: comparing Kelly, Croker, and Murphy to Washington and Lincoln, Plunkitt argued that boss-run government at least ran—"The machinery moves so noiseless that you wouldn't think there was any"—while bossless reform administrations dissolved into squabbling.

The Machine as Welfare State: Merton's Latent Functions

For half a century after Riordon's book appeared, most educated Americans treated the machine as simply a pathology. The sociologist Robert K. Merton, in a celebrated section of Social Theory and Social Structure (1949), asked the question that moralism had obscured: if the machine was so bad, why wouldn't it die? Machines, he observed, had shown a "phoenix-like" capacity to rise from every defeat. His answer relied on the distinction between manifest functions—the intended, recognized purposes of an institution—and latent functions, the unintended, unrecognized work it actually performs. Judged by civics-book ideals of honest, merit-based government, the machine was indefensible. But a persistent structure, Merton argued, ordinarily persists because it is "satisfying basic latent functions" that approved institutions leave unfilled. The boss's key function was to "organize, centralize and maintain in good working condition 'the scattered fragments of power'" that American constitutional design—separated powers, weak mayors, fragmented boards—deliberately dispersed. Where official government could not act, the machine could.

Merton then identified the subgroups served. For the "deprived classes," the machine supplied personalized welfare—his prose converging almost exactly with Plunkitt's fire-engine anecdote:

The precinct captain is forever a friend in need. In our prevailingly impersonal society, the machine, through its local agents, fulfills the important social function of humanizing and personalizing all manner of assistance to those in need... in contrast to the professional techniques of the welfare worker which may typically represent in the mind of the recipient the cold, bureaucratic dispensation of limited aid following upon detailed investigation... are the unprofessional techniques of the precinct captain who asks no questions, exacts no compliance with legal rules of eligibility and does not "snoop" into private affairs.

— Robert K. Merton, Social Theory and Social Structure (1949)

Merton clinched the point with the Boston ward boss Martin Lomasney's remark to Lincoln Steffens: "there's got to be in every ward somebody that any bloke can come to—no matter what he's done—and get help. Help, you understand; none of your law and justice, but help!" For business, second, the boss provided the privileges—franchises, contracts, protection from "a chaos of uncontrolled competition"—that firms wanted but could not openly obtain; he was "the business community's ambassador" in the alien realm of government. Third, the machine offered channels of social mobility for immigrants and their children, largely shut out of banks, law firms, and corporate offices. Fourth—a function nobody advertised—it performed the same brokerage for "illegitimate business," vice and gambling.

Merton was emphatic that this analysis was "not 'in support of the political machine.'" It was a warning to reformers: abolishing the machine without alternative structures to meet the needs it served would fail, "on pain of suffering acute disappointments and boomerang effects." The prediction held up. Machines weakened not when they were exposed but when their functions were taken over—when New Deal relief, civil service expansion, and social insurance began doing bureaucratically what the precinct captain had done personally, and when restricted immigration and rising prosperity thinned the ranks of voters who needed a friend.

Key Concept: Manifest vs. Latent Functions

Merton's distinction converts a moral question—"Is boss rule wicked?"—into an analytical one: "What needs does it satisfy, for whom, and what would have to replace it?" It remains a standard tool for analyzing why "dysfunctional" institutions endure.

Machines, Jobs, and the Origins of Public-Sector Labor

The currency in which the machine paid its workers was, above all, the public payroll—which is why machine politics is inseparable from the early history of public employment. As the historian Joseph E. Hower shows, government workers had been organizing since the Jacksonian era: navy-yard craftsmen lobbied for shorter hours in 1835, President Van Buren ordered a ten-hour day for federal manual laborers in 1840, and Congress passed a prevailing-wage law for navy projects in 1861. But for municipal workers in the machine era, Hower notes, "political agitation, not workplace action, proved the most effective vehicle"—the road to a raise ran through the ward boss, not a union. He quotes the labor economist John R. Commons on New York: "If the Knights of Labor wanted to secure an advantage for any member, they went to the Tammany politician, who went to the commissioner of street cleaning, or to the mayor, and secured the desired promotion, or relaxation of discipline or release from punishment." The machine was a crude grievance procedure—one more latent function for Merton's list.

This is exactly the world civil service reform set out to destroy. The Pendleton Act of 1883, passed after a disappointed office-seeker assassinated President Garfield, began substituting competitive examinations for patronage in federal hiring; state and municipal versions followed. Plunkitt's talks return to the subject obsessively, because it struck at the machine's lifeblood. "This civil service law is the biggest fraud of the age," he declared. "It is the curse of the nation... How are you goin' to interest our young men in their country if you have no offices to give them when they work for their party?" His examples are burlesque—the "fool questions" about mummies; the patriotic young Flaherty who, blasted by an examination, ended up dead at San Juan Hill in a Spanish uniform—but his syllogism was serious: parties built the government; parties cannot survive without offices to distribute; kill patronage and you kill party government. Even his support for municipal ownership of utilities was conditional on repealing civil service first: "Just see how many thousands of new places there would be for the workers in Tammany." Against constitutional objections, he quoted a colleague's shrug: "What's the constitution among friends?"

Hower's account shows the deeper irony: civil service reform, designed to depoliticize public work, ended up politicizing public workers. Once protected from patronage turnover, employees began treating their jobs as careers worth organizing to improve. Postal workers built genuine unions in the late nineteenth century, provoking a federal "gag rule"—begun in 1895 and extended by Theodore Roosevelt in 1902—that barred employees from lobbying Congress. Margaret Haley and Chicago teachers founded the Chicago Teachers' Federation in 1897. President Taft stated the emerging doctrine in 1911: government employees were "a privileged class" upon whom conditions could be imposed "that should not be and ought not be imposed upon those who serve private employers." Unionized public workers more than doubled between 1915 and 1921—but the surge proved what the historian Joseph Slater has called a "false dawn." In September 1919, after the police commissioner refused to let officers affiliate with the American Federation of Labor, most of the Boston police force walked out. Days of rioting followed; every striker was fired; and Governor Calvin Coolidge rode to national fame declaring there was "no right to strike against the public safety by anybody, anywhere, any time." The strike froze public-sector unionism for a generation, and it marks the outer edge of this chapter's story.

The Reform Critique: A Preview

Plunkitt's "mornin' glories" gibe was fair through about 1901, but as Connolly emphasizes, the reformers ultimately reshaped city government even where they never dislodged the machine. Municipal reform drew on many constituencies—businessmen seeking efficiency, women activists practicing "municipal housekeeping," labor and ethnic leaders with their own grievances—and left a durable legacy: civil service commissions, secret ballots, voter registration, nonpartisan and at-large elections, city commissions and managers. In the younger cities of the West and Southwest, where parties had never entrenched themselves, business elites used these Progressive devices to consolidate their own power. Connolly's sobering conclusion is that by 1940, whether a city was run by a reform regime or a party machine, its politics had grown more centralized and "less responsive to the concerns and demands of workers and immigrants." Reform was not simply democracy's victory over corruption; it often traded the machine's rough, transactional responsiveness for government by experts accountable to a narrower public. Whether that trade was worth making—whether the Plunkitts or the Progressives better served the American city—is the midterm debate, and Chapter 5 examines the reformers on their own terms.

Social Scientists, Social Reformers: Ideas and Institutions of the Progressive Era

How a generation of economists, settlement workers, photographers, sociologists, and businessmen turned the industrial city into a problem to be investigated — and fought over who would solve it, and for whom.

Learning Objectives

  • Explain Henry George's paradox of "progress and poverty" and the reasoning behind the single tax on land values.
  • Describe the settlement house idea as Jane Addams practiced it at Hull-House, including its blend of neighborly residence, cultural exchange, and systematic investigation.
  • Analyze how Jacob Riis used flash photography, statistics, and moral narrative in How the Other Half Lives to make tenement conditions politically visible.
  • Evaluate W.E.B. Du Bois's The Philadelphia Negro as a founding work of empirical urban sociology, and identify its central argument about environment versus individual failing.
  • Summarize Maureen Flanagan's case that there was no single Progressive movement but multiple, sometimes conflicting "progressivisms."
  • Assess Samuel P. Hays's revisionist argument that structural municipal reform was an upper-class, business-led project of centralization — and weigh it against the reformers' own self-descriptions.

In September 1889, two college-educated women moved into a battered mansion on Halsted Street in Chicago, wedged — as a local wit put it — between an undertaker and a saloon: "Knight, Death and the Devil." In 1887, a Danish-born police reporter in New York read that German chemists had found a way to take photographs by flashlight, and realized he could now drag the darkest tenement interiors into public view. In 1896, a twenty-eight-year-old Black scholar with a Harvard doctorate began knocking on doors in Philadelphia's Seventh Ward, and did not stop until he had personally interviewed thousands of households. And behind all of them stood a book published in 1879 by a self-taught San Francisco printer, which asked the question that haunted the whole era: if American cities were generating more wealth than any society in human history, why were they also generating more poverty?

This chapter follows the men and women who invented new ways of knowing the industrial city — the survey, the settlement, the flash photograph, the statistical map — and the institutions they built to act on that knowledge. It ends with two historians, Maureen Flanagan and Samuel P. Hays, who force us to ask harder questions: Who exactly were these "Progressives"? And when reformers rewrote the rules of city government in the name of honesty and efficiency, who actually gained power, and who lost it?

The Paradox of Progress and Poverty: Henry George

Henry George was not a professor. He had been a sailor, a gold-rush prospector, a typesetter, and a struggling San Francisco newspaper editor before he published Progress and Poverty in 1879. The book became one of the best-selling works of political economy of the nineteenth century, and its opening chapter, "The Problem," frames the question that every reformer in this chapter tried, in one way or another, to answer.

George begins with the promise of the age. "The present century has been marked by a prodigious increase in wealth-producing power," he writes: steam, electricity, labor-saving machinery, the division of labor on a grand scale. A man of Benjamin Franklin's generation, shown a vision of the steamship and the railroad and the factory, would surely have inferred that poverty was about to disappear — that these "muscles of iron and sinews of steel" would make "the poorest laborer's life a holiday." Yet the opposite had happened. From every industrialized nation came reports of depression, unemployment, and want. And crucially, George observed, the distress was worst not where progress had lagged but where it was most advanced. In raw new frontier communities there was no luxury but also no destitution; in the great cities, wealth and misery grew side by side. "The 'tramp' comes with the locomotive, and almshouses and prisons are as surely the marks of 'material progress' as are costly dwellings, rich warehouses, and magnificent churches."

The new forces, elevating in their nature though they be, do not act upon the social fabric from underneath, as was for a long time hoped and believed, but strike it at a point intermediate between top and bottom. It is as though an immense wedge were being forced, not underneath society, but through society. Those who are above the point of separation are elevated, but those who are below are crushed down.

— Henry George, Progress and Poverty (1879)

"This association of poverty with progress is the great enigma of our times," George concluded — "the riddle which the Sphinx of Fate puts to our civilization, and which not to answer is to be destroyed." To educate men condemned to poverty "is but to make them restive"; to build formally equal political institutions atop glaring inequality "is to stand a pyramid on its apex." His answer, developed over the rest of the book, located the problem in land. As cities grew, the value of urban land soared — not because its owners did anything, but because the whole community grew around it. Landowners captured this "unearned increment" as rent, while speculation in land drove up housing costs and periodically crashed the economy. George's remedy was the single tax: shift all taxation onto the value of land itself, socializing the community-created gain while leaving buildings, labor, and capital untaxed.

The single tax movement never captured national policy, but its influence was enormous. George nearly rode it into office: running for mayor of New York in 1886 on a labor ticket, he finished second, ahead of a young Republican named Theodore Roosevelt. More important for our purposes, Progress and Poverty gave an entire generation its problem statement. Jane Addams's Chicago circle debated George; British admirers founded land-reform leagues; and the conviction that poverty was a product of social arrangements rather than personal sin — that it could be studied, explained, and remedied — became the founding premise of Progressive social science.

Key Concept: The Single Tax

George argued that the rising value of land — especially urban land — is created by the community's growth, not by the owner's effort. Taxing away that "unearned increment" (and abolishing taxes on labor and capital) would, he believed, end speculation, lower rents, and eliminate involuntary poverty. The idea survives today in land-value taxation debates and in the anti-monopoly board game that began life as "The Landlord's Game," designed by a Georgist.

The Settlement Idea: Jane Addams and Hull-House

If George supplied the diagnosis, the settlement house supplied a distinctive institutional response: not a charity dispensing relief from above, but a residence — educated men and women moving into a poor neighborhood to live there, permanently, as neighbors. The model was Toynbee Hall, founded in London's East End in 1884. Jane Addams and Ellen Gates Starr carried it to Chicago, and on September 18, 1889, moved into the old Hull mansion at Polk and Halsted Streets, in the heart of the immigrant Nineteenth Ward. By 1910, when Addams published her memoir Twenty Years at Hull-House, the settlement had grown to thirteen buildings, and more than four hundred settlements had opened across the United States.

Addams's account of the founding is disarmingly practical — furniture, an unlocked door on the first night, a haunted attic — but beneath it lies a carefully worked-out theory. "Hull-House was soberly opened," she writes, "on the theory that the dependence of classes on each other is reciprocal." The settlement was meant to benefit the settlers as much as the neighborhood. In her famous companion essay on the "subjective necessity for social settlements" (Chapter 6 of the memoir), Addams argued that educated young people — especially educated young women, barred from most professions — were starving for real contact with the world and real work to do. The settlement rescued them from what she called elsewhere "the snare of preparation" even as it served the ward.

The blessings which we associate with a life of refinement and cultivation can be made universal and must be made universal if they are to be permanent; ... the good we secure for ourselves is precarious and uncertain, is floating in mid-air, until it is secured for all of us and incorporated into our common life.

— Jane Addams, Twenty Years at Hull-House (1910)

Addams was unsparing about what she found on Halsted Street: streets "inexpressibly dirty," schools inadequate, "sanitary legislation unenforced," hundreds of houses unconnected to sewers, and a sweating system in which "an unscrupulous contractor regards no basement as too dark, no stable loft too foul ... for his workroom." Her chapter on the problems of poverty records the human texture of these conditions — the old woman clinging bodily to her chest of drawers rather than be taken to the poorhouse; Mrs. Moran, whose relief flour burst in the rain and who "roundly cursed poverty" itself; the desperate winter after the World's Fair of 1893, when unemployed men slept in the corridors of City Hall.

What distinguished Hull-House from older charity, however, was its insistence that sympathy be disciplined by evidence. Addams demanded of settlement residents "a scientific patience in the accumulation of facts." When the ward's garbage-choked alleys and soaring death rate became intolerable, the Hull-House Woman's Club did not merely petition: twelve members walked the alleys three evenings a week through the summer heat, and in two months sent 1,037 substantiated reports of health-law violations to city hall. When nothing changed, Addams submitted her own bid for the ward's garbage-removal contract — and though the bid was thrown out on a technicality, the mayor appointed her ward garbage inspector, a salaried political "plum" whose loss enraged the local machine. She rose at six to follow the wagons, hauled landlords into court, and saw the ward's death rate drop from third to seventh in the city. The alderman retaliated by abolishing her position. Investigation, publicity, legislation, and political conflict: the whole Progressive repertoire in miniature.

The same method produced Hull-House Maps and Papers (1895), the settlement's block-by-block survey of nationalities and wages in the ward — the first great social survey of an American neighborhood, built from data a resident collected for the U.S. Bureau of Labor's investigation of "the slums of great cities." Hull-House investigations of the sweating system helped drive Illinois's pioneering factory legislation, and its alumnae — Florence Kelley, Julia Lathrop, Alice Hamilton — carried the survey method into state factory inspection, the federal Children's Bureau, and industrial medicine.

Key Concept: The Settlement House

A settlement was a residence, not an agency: educated volunteers "settled" in poor urban neighborhoods to share daily life with their neighbors, offering kindergartens, clubs, English classes, art, and meeting space, while studying conditions and lobbying for reform. Addams insisted the relation was reciprocal — the settlement met the "subjective necessity" of idle, educated youth as much as the objective needs of the ward. By 1910 there were more than four hundred settlements in American cities.

Seeing the Other Half: Jacob Riis and the Camera

Jacob Riis arrived from Denmark in 1870 at age twenty-one and spent his first years in America close to the bottom — sleeping at times in the police lodging-houses he would later persuade Theodore Roosevelt to close. As a police reporter headquartered on Mulberry Street, he knew the tenement districts intimately; what he lacked was a way to make comfortable New Yorkers see them. The answer came in 1887, when he read of a German invention: magnesium flash powder, which made photography possible in the darkest airshaft bedroom or stale-beer dive. Riis and his collaborators began raiding the slums by night, camera and flash pan in hand; the images went first into lantern-slide lectures, then into a magazine article, and in 1890 into a book whose title became a permanent phrase in the American language.

Long ago it was said that "one half of the world does not know how the other half lives." That was true then. It did not know because it did not care. The half that was on top cared little for the struggles, and less for the fate of those who were underneath, so long as it was able to hold them there and keep its own seat.

— Jacob Riis, How the Other Half Lives (1890)

How the Other Half Lives is at once statistics and sermon. Riis reported that three-fourths of New York's people now lived in tenements — thirty-seven thousand buildings housing more than 1.2 million persons — and traced the "genesis of the tenement" to simple greed: old merchant houses partitioned into warrens, rear houses stacked in former gardens, "the dark bedroom, prolific of untold depravities." His tour of the districts moves ethnic group by ethnic group — the Italians of Mulberry Bend ("foul core of New York's slums"), the Jewish garment workers of "Jewtown" and its sweaters, the Bohemian cigarmakers manufacturing in their own bedrooms, the working girls, the paupers, the tramps. Modern readers will be struck, and should be troubled, by Riis's freehanded ethnic stereotyping; he shared many of his era's prejudices even as he indicted its indifference. Yet in "The Color Line in New York" his structural instinct cuts through: Black New Yorkers, he showed, were confined by landlords who "make the prejudice in which he traffics pay him well" — "the Czar of all the Russias is not more absolute upon his own soil than the New York landlord in his dealings with colored tenants" — and were charged higher rents than any white group for the worst rooms.

Riis's conclusion, "How the Case Stands," is a reformer's brief: the tenement "has come to stay," so it must be made decent — by law, by remodeling, and by philanthropic model tenements returning a limited dividend. Reform, he insisted, "must aim at making it unprofitable to own a bad tenement." His closing image is a warning drawn from the draft riots of 1863: "The sea of a mighty population, held in galling fetters, heaves uneasily in the tenements." The book's impact was immediate. Theodore Roosevelt, then a rising politician, left his card at Riis's office with the message that he had come to help; as police board president he toured the slums at Riis's side, and later pronounced him — in a phrase Riis treasured — the most useful citizen of New York. Riis's campaigns helped raze Mulberry Bend (replaced by a park in 1897) and fed the movement that produced New York's landmark Tenement House Act of 1901. He had demonstrated something every reformer after him would exploit: in urban politics, the image is an argument.

Key Concept: Muckraking and Photojournalism

Riis pioneered the exposé style — documentary fact plus moral outrage, amplified by new technology (flash powder, the halftone press, the lantern-slide lecture) — that a later generation of magazine writers such as Lincoln Steffens (The Shame of the Cities) turned on municipal corruption. Theodore Roosevelt named the genre "muckraking" in 1906, borrowing the man with the muck-rake from Pilgrim's Progress — the same figure George had invoked in 1879.

Counting and Mapping: W.E.B. Du Bois and the Science of the Survey

Where Riis relied on the camera and Addams on residence, W.E.B. Du Bois built his case with the schedule, the interview, and the map. In 1896 the University of Pennsylvania hired the young scholar — the first African American to earn a Harvard Ph.D. — as an "assistant in sociology" for a single task: to study the city's Black population, concentrated in the Seventh Ward. Reform-minded sponsors connected to the College Settlement expected a study of Negro criminality and vice; Du Bois delivered something far larger. From August 1896 to January 1898 he conducted, in the words of the book's introduction, "a house-to-house visitation" through which he "came in personal contact with over ten thousand Negro inhabitants of the city," attending churches, meetings, and businesses, mapping every household — assisted only by Isabel Eaton, whose companion study covered domestic service. The result, The Philadelphia Negro (1899), is generally regarded as the first scientific sociological study of an American urban community.

Du Bois was strikingly candid about method. Sociology's tools, he warned, were "so liable to inaccuracies" that the investigator "must ever tremble lest some personal bias ... has to a degree distorted the picture." But the answer to imperfect knowledge was more investigation, not less: "We must study, we must investigate, we must attempt to solve." His chapters on occupations (Sections 21–22 were assigned this week) show the machinery at work. Of the 257 working boys aged ten to twenty in the Seventh Ward, he counted 39 percent porters and errand boys, 25.5 percent servants, 16 percent common laborers — a population locked almost entirely out of skilled trades, a pattern the adult male workforce repeated on a larger scale (Figure 5.1). The cause, he argued, was double: the legacy of slavery in training and habits, which he did not minimize, and a "wide reaching discrimination" that shut the doors of advancement regardless of talent. His analytic conclusion deserves quotation, because it states the founding insight of environmental social science:

In the realm of social phenomena the law of survival is greatly modified by human choice, wish, whim and prejudice. And consequently one never knows when one sees a social outcast how far this failure to survive is due to the deficiencies of the individual, and how far to the accidents or injustice of his environment.

— W.E.B. Du Bois, The Philadelphia Negro (1899)

Du Bois insisted that Black Philadelphia was not a mass but a differentiated community with its own classes — it should not be judged by its "submerged tenth" — and that its people paid abnormally high rents for the poorest housing. His chapters on Negro suffrage (Sections 52–54) applied the same even-handed empiricism to politics. Philadelphia, he wrote acidly, had a government "unparalleled in the history of republican government for brazen dishonesty," and the machine had made Black voters its clients just as it had once made the Irish the Democrats' clients. Yet the ballot had won Black citizens real protections — access to the streetcars, a measure of leverage against oppression — and Black officeholders proved on average "good and much higher than one might naturally expect." In Section 55, "The Paradox of Reform," he anticipated the central tension of this chapter: respectable Black voters agreed that city politics were corrupt, but the very reformers who solicited their votes "will not themselves work beside Negroes, or admit them to positions in their stores or offices." Why should Black Philadelphians sacrifice the machine's tangible jobs and honors to gratify reformers who ignored them? "When election-day comes, the 'machine' gets their votes." Municipal reform, Du Bois showed, looked very different from the bottom of the labor market.

The Philadelphia study launched a program. From Atlanta University, where he taught from 1897, Du Bois proposed studying Black life city by city and directed the annual Atlanta Conferences — the "Atlanta School" of sociology, which practiced rigorous empirical urban research years before the more celebrated Chicago School, though white academia long refused it recognition or funding.

Who Were the Progressives? Flanagan and the Many Progressivisms

Textbooks once spoke confidently of "the Progressive movement." The historian Maureen Flanagan, in her synthesis "Progressives and Progressivism in an Era of Reform" (2019, Oxford Research Encyclopedia of American History), argues that the definite article is the problem. The decades from the 1890s into the 1920s did produce sweeping reforms, driven — she writes — by "a pervasive sense that the country's democratic promise was failing": endemic political corruption, an unregulated industrial economy whose legal system "protected the rights of business over labor," mass urbanization with its "poverty, disease, crime, and cultural clash." But the people who responded agreed on almost nothing — not on "the exact threat that confronted the nation, the means to resolve problems, or how to implement reforms." What united them, in Flanagan's account, was a temperament rather than a program: "all so-called Progressive reformers were modernizers," seeking a via media — a middle way between nineteenth-century laissez-faire liberalism and the radical alternatives of socialism or social democracy — and their combined efforts legitimated an activist state.

Hence historians now speak of multiple progressivisms: political reformers attacking bosses and rewriting charters; economic regulators pursuing trusts, railroads, and workplace safety; social-justice progressives — the Addams wing — building settlements and campaigning for labor legislation; moral reformers pursuing prohibition and vice crusades; and environmental reformers fighting for sanitation, parks, and clean water. These camps overlapped, but they also collided: the businessman's "efficient" city and the settlement worker's "caring" city could be direct rivals for the same tax dollars and the same charter provisions.

Flanagan's own research made gender central to this picture. Excluded from the vote through most of the era, urban women built a parallel politics through clubs, settlements, and consumers' leagues, justifying public activism as municipal housekeeping — the argument that if the city was a household, then garbage, milk inspection, smoke, schools, and playgrounds were women's business. Addams made the case exactly this way: if it was womanly to nurse the sick in tenements, "it might be quite as womanly to go through the same district in order to prevent the breeding of so-called 'filth diseases.'" In her study of Chicago, Seeing with Their Hearts, Flanagan showed that organized women often pushed a distinctive agenda — municipal ownership, public welfare, city services oriented to homes and children — that diverged sharply from the business-minded reform of their male counterparts in the City Club. Black reformers, meanwhile, built their own progressivism — Ida B. Wells's anti-lynching crusade, the settlement-like institutions of Black clubwomen, the NAACP (co-founded in 1909 by Du Bois) — usually excluded from, and often opposed by, white reform organizations. There was no one Progressive movement because there was no one vision of the good city.

What Was Reform Really About? Hays and the Revisionist Turn

The reformers of this chapter told a flattering story about themselves: "the people" versus "the interests," civic virtue versus the boss. For decades historians largely repeated it, or — like Richard Hofstadter in The Age of Reform (1955) — modified it into a tale of an anxious old middle class defending its status. In 1964, in the Pacific Northwest Quarterly, Samuel P. Hays published a short article that upended the field: "The Politics of Reform in Municipal Government in the Progressive Era." Hays's method was deceptively simple. Stop reading reformers' speeches, he urged, and start examining who the reformers actually were — their occupations, their organizations, their opponents, and the practical consequences of the changes they sought. This "social analysis" of political life produced a startling conclusion. In city after city — Hays drew on Pittsburgh, Des Moines, and the spread of the Galveston-born commission plan — the leadership of structural municipal reform came not from the middle class, still less from the masses, but from chambers of commerce, leading business groups, and the most advanced professionals, men "who sought to apply their specialized knowledge to a wider range of public affairs."

The movement for reform in municipal government, therefore, constituted an attempt by upper-class, advanced professional, and large business groups to take formal political power from the previously dominant lower- and middle-class elements so that they might advance their own conceptions of desirable public policy.

— Samuel P. Hays, The Politics of Reform in Municipal Government in the Progressive Era (1964)

The key evidence lay in the reforms themselves. The signature structural innovations of the era — the commission plan pioneered in Galveston after the 1900 hurricane, and the city-manager plan launched in Staunton, Virginia (1908) and made famous in Dayton, Ohio (1913) — replaced mayors and ward-based councils with small boards elected at large and professional administrators hired like corporate executives. Reformers described this as taking politics out of city government: running the city, as the slogan went, on business principles. Hays read the same changes structurally. Ward elections had given working-class and immigrant neighborhoods direct representation — their own alderman, their own recognizable stake in city hall. At-large elections and appointed managers shifted decision-making upward, to men who could command citywide reputations and citywide campaign funds. Reform, in Hays's telling, was not the extension of democracy but the centralization of it: business elites believed the city's welfare "would be best achieved if the business community controlled the city government." That lower- and middle-class wards frequently voted against commission and manager charters — a fact the older historiography simply ignored — was, for Hays, the decisive tell. The ideology of reform ("the public interest," "efficiency," "nonpartisanship") was universalist; its practice redistributed power to a class.

Hays's article became a foundation of the new urban political history, and it reframed the machine as well as the reformer: if reform was class politics from above, the boss's exchange of jobs, favors, and recognition for votes looked less like simple parasitism and more like representation — crude, corrupt, but responsive — for people the reformed city would not represent. Du Bois's Seventh Ward voters, weighing the machine's tangible jobs against reformers who would not hire them, had made exactly this calculation in 1897. Yet Hays should sharpen, not settle, our judgment. His argument fits structural reform — charters, commissions, managers — far better than it fits the social progressivism of Addams, Kelley, and the municipal housekeepers, who sought to expand what the city did for its poorest wards, not to shrink their voice. Flanagan's multiple progressivisms and Hays's class analysis together leave us with the era's enduring question: "reform" is never simply good government; it is always also a claim about who should govern.

Discussion Questions

  2. Addams claimed the settlement met a "subjective necessity" of the educated as much as the objective needs of the poor. Is this honesty a strength of the settlement idea or a symptom of its limits? How did residence change what Hull-House could know and do compared with older charity?
  3. Riis's photographs moved legislatures, but his chapters traffic in ethnic stereotype even while indicting landlords and public neglect. How should historians weigh the reformer's methods and prejudices against the reforms he achieved?
  4. Du Bois wrote that one never knows how far a "social outcast" has failed through personal deficiency "and how far [through] the accidents or injustice of his environment." Can both descriptions be true? Test Hays's thesis against Addams's garbage-inspector fight and Du Bois's "paradox of reform" among Black voters.

A Saturday in 1905

Having argued out the merits of boss politicians and Progressive reformers in last week's midterm debate, we turn now from the city as a problem of governance to the city as a landscape of desire. Follow a twenty-year-old garment worker — call her Rose — through a New York Saturday in 1905. In the morning she rides a streetcar downtown and drifts through the marble-and-plate-glass palace of a department store, fingering shirtwaists she cannot afford, welcome nonetheless in a space built to flatter her as a "lady." In the evening she pays a dime at a Bowery dance hall, where a young man she has never met "treats" her to soda and a turn on the floor. And on Sunday — her one full day of freedom — she joins several hundred thousand others riding excursion boats and elevated trains to Coney Island, where a fantastic city of towers and electric fire rises from the sand.

Every stop on Rose's itinerary was new. In 1840, American cities offered little commercial pleasure beyond the tavern and the occasional theater; respectable women scarcely appeared in public unescorted, and the night belonged to watchmen and rowdies. By 1905 an entire urban economy had been built around work's opposite: selling fun by the ticket, the drink, the dance, and the ride. This chapter examines that revolution — where it happened, who claimed it, and who was fenced out by the color line.

Making Downtown: The Central Business District

The historian Emily Remus, in her overview of the central business district for the Oxford Research Encyclopedia of American History (2019), reminds us that "downtown" was not a natural feature of cities but a historical creation. In the walking city of the early nineteenth century, merchants lived above their countinghouses and workshops mingled with residences. Between the 1840s and the 1890s, rising land values, horsecars, and then electric streetcars and elevated railroads sorted urban space by function: production moved toward rail corridors and waterfronts, the well-to-do decamped toward the suburbs, and a compact core emerged devoted to commerce, retail, and entertainment — "downtown" in New York, "the Loop" in Chicago. Steel-frame construction and the passenger elevator, pioneered in the 1880s, stacked offices into skyscrapers and concentrated tens of thousands of clerks and managers on a few hundred acres that pulsed with commuters by day and emptied by night. Figure 6.1 sketches the anatomy of this new district.

The institution that anchored downtown's retail heart was the department store. Alexander T. Stewart's "Marble Palace" in New York pointed the way at midcentury, but the classic emporium matured in the 1870s and 1880s: John Wanamaker converted a Philadelphia freight depot into his "Grand Depot" in 1876, and Marshall Field built a Chicago establishment whose creed — give the lady what she wants — became retailing legend. These stores were consumption engines dressed as civic monuments: fixed prices and free entry replaced haggling, plate-glass windows turned sidewalks into galleries, and tearooms, lounges, and deferential clerks made shopping an occupation in itself. Crucially, as Remus shows in A Shoppers' Paradise (2019), the department store engineered a revolution in urban gender geography. Downtown had been a masculine terrain of exchanges, offices, and saloons where an unescorted woman risked her reputation. The great stores, with their satellite theaters and tearooms, gave affluent and middling women a legitimate public claim on the city's core — and when women with money asserted that claim, they collided with businessmen and moralists who fought over saloon locations, sidewalk behavior, and whether female pleasure-seeking belonged in the city's most valuable square mile. The struggle over downtown, Remus argues, was a struggle over its moral geography: who belonged where, doing what.

Downtown also fed people. As Cindy Lobel shows in her work on food in the nineteenth-century city, when clerks and shopgirls could no longer walk home for dinner, a commercial food landscape sprang up to meet them: oyster cellars below sidewalk level, saloons offering a "free lunch" with a nickel beer, lunch counters timed to the office clock, and, at the summit, restaurants like Delmonico's, where the elite conducted politics over terrapin. Eating out — like shopping, once suspect for respectable women — became an ordinary urban habit, stratified by price into a legible map of class.

Key Concept: Moral Geography

Historians use "moral geography" for the mental maps by which city people assigned virtue and vice to particular spaces — and particular bodies in them. A middle-class woman in a department store at 2 p.m. was a "shopper"; alone on the same block at 11 p.m., she was presumed to be something else entirely. Commercial leisure constantly redrew these maps; reformers constantly fought to redraw them back.

The Lit City at Night

If downtown was commercial leisure's place, night became its time. Peter C. Baldwin, historian of the nocturnal city, argues that for most of the nineteenth century respectable opinion regarded the urban night as morally dangerous almost by definition: darkness sheltered thieves, gamblers, and prostitutes, and decent people were expected to be home behind shutters. Two technologies broke that regime. Gas lighting, introduced in the 1810s and 1820s, first pushed back the dark along main thoroughfares; from the late 1870s, arc lamps and Edison's incandescent system flooded shopping streets and theater districts with brilliant white light. Illuminated show windows, electric signs, and blazing marquees turned districts like Broadway — the "Great White Way" — into round-the-clock advertisements, and evening strolling became free entertainment in itself.

Onto this lit stage marched an expanding cast of night institutions. The saloon was the workingman's club — by the 1890s many neighborhoods counted one for every few hundred residents — offering warmth, newspapers, free lunch, and political connections with the beer. Concert saloons and variety halls added female waiters and bawdy song; cheap theaters and, after 1905, nickelodeons packed in families; around 1911 the cabaret seated well-to-do patrons at tables around a dance floor — respectable flirtation with what had recently been vice. Baldwin emphasizes that the night city was never conquered evenly: hours that meant pleasure for some meant labor for the scrubwomen, pressmen, and waiters who kept it running, and moral reformers followed the lights, censoring dance halls and — with Prohibition in 1920 — shutting the saloon altogether, driving nightlife into the speakeasy.

Night leisure also had an underground economy. Matthew Vaz's history of gambling in northern cities shows that betting — policy in the nineteenth century, the "numbers game" in the twentieth — was woven into everyday urban life, tolerated by police who taxed it through graft, and, in Black neighborhoods where legitimate credit was rationed, capable of making numbers bankers into some of the community's wealthiest figures. Gambling sat where this chapter's themes cross: commercial pleasure, shadow financial institution, and perpetual target of the reformers who policed the saloon and dance hall.

Cheap Amusements: Working Women and the New Heterosocial Culture

Who filled the dance halls and picture shows? Disproportionately, the young working women whose Saturday opened this chapter. In Cheap Amusements: Working Women and Leisure in Turn-of-the-Century New York (1986), Kathy Peiss recovered the leisure world of these daughters of immigrants — shirtwaist sewers, cigar rollers, shopgirls — and made a large claim for it. Between 1880 and 1920, she argued, urban sociability shifted from homosocial (men in saloons and lodges, women in kitchens and stoops) to heterosocial: young men and women mixing freely in commercial spaces built for that purpose. And this reorientation of American culture came substantially from below, pioneered in working-class dance halls and amusement parks decades before middle-class youth discovered "dating."

Peiss's chapter "Leisure and Labor" begins with the arithmetic of exploitation. Working women earned perhaps half of men's wages — often five or six dollars a week — and most surrendered their pay envelopes to their families. Yet industrial discipline also, paradoxically, created leisure: shorter and more regular hours than domestic service allowed, a clear boundary between the boss's time and one's own, and workplaces full of young women trading news of dances, fashions, and beaux. Unable to afford commercial pleasure on their own wages, many participated through "treating": accepting drinks, theater tickets, dance-hall admissions, and Coney Island excursions from male companions. Both parties understood the exchange to carry expectations — from flirtatious attention to sexual favors — and vice investigators used the name "charity girls" for young women who bestowed those favors for presents and a good time rather than money, distinguishing them sharply from prostitutes. Peiss reads treating neither as innocent nor as simple victimization, but as a bargain struck within brutal constraints: working women converted the one asset the leisure market prized — their company — into pleasures their pay could never buy, trading independence from watchful parents for a new dependence on men.

The dance hall was this culture's capital. Hundreds of halls, many attached to saloons or rented by immigrant clubs for "rackets," offered music, spieling (a dizzying close-hold whirl), and "tough dances" that scandalized chaperones. Reformers founded supervised recreation centers and campaigned to license the halls — one more front in the Progressive war to substitute uplift for commerce in the lives of the urban poor. The working girls mostly kept dancing.

Key Concept: Heterosocial Culture

"Heterosocial" describes leisure organized around the mixing of the sexes — dance halls, amusement parks, cabarets — as against the sex-segregated ("homosocial") world of the saloon and the sewing circle. Peiss argues that working-class youth built America's first mass heterosocial culture, which entrepreneurs then packaged and sold upward to the middle class.

The Ballpark: Baseball as a School for City People

Commercial leisure did not only pair off couples; it also assembled crowds — and taught them, some historians argue, how to be urban publics. In City People: The Rise of Modern City Culture in Nineteenth-Century America (1980), Gunther Barth examined a set of new institutions — the metropolitan press, the department store, the vaudeville house, and the ballpark — that, he argued, forged a shared modern culture out of the fragmented industrial city. His chapter "Ball Park" makes the case that professional baseball became the great democratizing spectacle of the nineteenth-century city. From the codification of the "New York game" by the Knickerbocker club in the 1840s to the founding of the National League in 1876, baseball evolved from a gentleman's exercise into a business of spectatorship — and what the paying crowd bought was more than a contest. In a city of strangers divided by language, class, and neighborhood, the ballpark offered a rare experience of commonality: tens of thousands of clerks, mechanics, and merchants judging the same umpire, mastering the same rules and statistics broadcast by the new sports page, and rehearsing — in their insistence that the game be played fairly and skill rewarded — a civics lesson in competitive, rule-bound modern life. Cheering was something a laborer and a bank teller could do together, on terms of rough equality.

Steven Riess's scholarship on professional team sports fills in the business beneath Barth's cultural portrait — and complicates its democratic glow. Major-league baseball was a syndicate of profit-seeking owners, frequently allied with traction magnates and machine politicians who supplied streetcar routes and police protection. The parks tracked the city's growth: wooden firetraps at the trolley lines' ends gave way, beginning in 1909 with Shibe Park and Forbes Field, to monumental steel-and-concrete stadiums — Ebbets Field, opened in Brooklyn in 1913, became the most beloved — that anchored their neighborhoods for half a century. And the grandstand's democracy had limits: ticket prices and Sunday-baseball bans filtered the crowd, women were courted only gingerly with "Ladies' Days," and after the 1880s the professional diamond was closed to Black players altogether, forcing Black baseball into the parallel institution-building that culminated in the Negro Leagues of the 1920s.

Coney Island and the Mechanics of Fun

At the far end of the excursion lines lay commercial leisure's laboratory. Coney Island, a beach resort at Brooklyn's ocean edge long notorious for saloons, racetracks, and gamblers, was remade between 1897 and 1904 by three great enclosed parks. George C. Tilyou's Steeplechase Park (1897) wrapped mechanical rides in participatory mischief: its gravity horse race let couples career around the park's rim, and its funhouse contraptions — blasts of air, collapsing floors, the human roulette wheel — made the crowd itself the show, dissolving Victorian body discipline in collective laughter. Frederic Thompson and Elmer "Skip" Dundy's Luna Park (1903) sold architectural delirium: a dream city of spires and lagoons traced at night by hundreds of thousands of electric bulbs, with simulated spectacles — a trip to the moon, tenement fires heroically extinguished on schedule — that turned urban modernity itself into a ride. Dreamland (1904) answered with a million lights and a 375-foot tower before burning down, with appropriate irony, in 1911. On peak Sundays the resort drew crowds in the hundreds of thousands; no institution in America mixed classes, sexes, and ethnicities so promiscuously — precisely why Peiss's working women loved it and moralists distrusted it. Visiting in 1906, Maxim Gorky found the spectacle magnificent and the pleasure hollow — his essay in The Independent (1907) was titled, witheringly, "Boredom" — but even he could not resist the view from the water:

With the advent of night a fantastic city all of fire suddenly rises from the ocean into the sky. Thousands of ruddy sparks glimmer in the darkness, limning in fine, sensitive outline on the black background of the sky, shapely towers of miraculous castles, palaces and temples. … Fabulous and beyond conceiving, ineffably beautiful, is this fiery scintillation.

— Maxim Gorky, "Boredom," The Independent (1907)

For a moving picture of this world — literally — we have Roscoe "Fatty" Arbuckle's two-reel comedy Coney Island (Paramount, 1917), shot on location in the parks and now in the public domain. The plot is a treating-culture farce: Fatty, dragged to the beach by his domineering wife, buries himself in the sand to escape her and sneaks into the park, where he, his wife's friend, and a young rival (a pre-stardom Buster Keaton) compete for a pretty girl's attention — buying her ice cream, squiring her onto the rides — until a log-flume soaking sends Fatty into a ladies' bathing costume and the tangle collapses into brawls, arrests, and a jailhouse finale in which the men swear off women and immediately chase two more. As history, the film is a document twice over. Its backgrounds preserve the working park in candid detail — the Witching Waves undulating, the Shoot-the-Chutes splashing down, the crowds in their Sunday best — and its comedy assumes everything this chapter has described: that married people, single girls, and predatory flirts shared one holiday crowd; that men paid and women were treated; that bodies at Coney Island escaped the rules that governed them on Monday morning. Audiences in 1917 needed none of this explained: the amusement park had become the shared vernacular of city life. Even its racism was casual — the film's original ending, cut from most prints by the mid-1920s, played Fatty's horror at flirting with a Black woman as a joke, a reminder that the "democratic" crowd was policed by the color line even at play.

Leisure Under the Color Line

That reminder frames our final reading. In 1896–97 the young W. E. B. Du Bois conducted a door-to-door survey of Philadelphia's Seventh Ward, published as The Philadelphia Negro (1899) — the first great empirical study of an American urban community. Section 46, "Social Classes and Amusements," asks how Black Philadelphians actually spent their leisure, opening with a blow against the era's racism:

There is always a strong tendency on the part of the community to consider the Negroes as composing one practically homogeneous mass. … [Y]et if the foregoing statistics have emphasized any one fact it is that wide variations in antecedents, wealth, intelligence and general efficiency have already been differentiated within this group.

— W. E. B. Du Bois, The Philadelphia Negro (1899)

Du Bois sorted the ward's 2,395 Black families into four grades — a small well-to-do elite; a broad respectable working class; the poor; and a "submerged tenth" of criminals and prostitutes — each with its own leisure world. The lowest grades frequented the gambling clubs and pool-rooms around Seventh and Lombard streets, along with balls, cake-walks, and rowdy summer excursions. The laboring majority — the porters, waiters, and servant girls of Black Philadelphia — took their amusements "in connection with the churches": suppers, fairs, concerts, and Sunday-school excursions, since many congregations forbade dancing and the theater. The small upper class, barred by white prejudice from the city's hotels, restaurants, and resorts, turned inward, making the private home the center of its recreation.

The pattern was not a matter of taste but of exclusion. Du Bois defined Philadelphia's color prejudice, in the very next chapter, as the feeling that shut Black citizens out of "certain public conveniences and amusements" as surely as out of decent employment. The commercial leisure economy this chapter has described — the tearoom, the cabaret, the grandstand, the dance pavilion — either barred Black patrons outright or humiliated them with segregated corners. What Du Bois saw in consequence was an institutional vacuum, named with a reformer's urgency:

Among nearly all classes of Negroes there is a large unsatisfied demand for amusement. Large numbers of servant girls and young men have flocked to the city, have no homes, and want places to frequent. The churches supply this need partially, but the institution which will supply this want better and add instruction and diversion, will save many girls from ruin and boys from crime.

— W. E. B. Du Bois, The Philadelphia Negro, §46 (1899)

Here is Peiss's problem — young migrants, wretched wages, and a hunger for pleasure — refracted through the color line: Rose could at least spend her suitor's dime at the dance hall, while her Black counterpart in domestic service found even that door closed or dangerous. In the decades after Du Bois wrote, Black urbanites answered the vacuum by building a parallel leisure world — Black-owned theaters, dance halls, resorts, and baseball clubs, financed not infrequently (as Vaz shows) by numbers bankers — institutional creativity under constraint that anticipates the Great Migration cities of coming weeks.

Key Concept: Commercialized Leisure

Historians use "commercial leisure" (or Peiss's "cheap amusements") for the profit-making pleasure institutions — saloons, dance halls, theaters, ballparks, amusement parks — that displaced recreation rooted in church, family, and ethnic mutuality between roughly 1870 and 1920, moving leisure out of the home, pricing it by the ticket, mixing the sexes and (unevenly) the classes, and making "fun" one of the city's biggest industries.

Conclusion: The City as Playground — for Whom?

Between 1840 and 1920 American cities invented modern fun. The sorting of urban space created a downtown where consumption reigned and women shoppers claimed public life; gas and electricity annexed the night for commerce; industrial wages and time-discipline gave even the poorly paid a sliver of money and hours to spend; and entrepreneurs from Wanamaker to Tilyou built machinery to collect both. Our authors disagree about what it meant. Barth saw new institutions teaching a fragmented population how to be one urban public; Peiss saw young women bargaining for autonomy inside a market that also exploited them; Gorky saw a glittering swindle; Du Bois saw a pleasure economy whose gates were guarded by race. All understood that leisure had become a serious historical force — as revealing of urban power as the factory, the tenement, or the political machine. The struggles over parks, playgrounds, and public space in the twentieth-century city are, in large part, struggles over who inherited the world this chapter describes.

Order and Disorder: Riots, Vice, and the Invention of the Police

How urban rioting, crusades against drink and commercial sex, and anxieties about race and immigration produced the municipal police — and left them a dilemma never resolved.

The Rioting City

On October 21, 1835, a Boston mob several thousand strong seized the abolitionist editor William Lloyd Garrison, looped a rope around him, and dragged him through the streets toward what many intended to be a lynching. The mayor's men hustled Garrison into jail — for his own protection, since the city had no force capable of dispersing the crowd. Boston was two centuries old; it had a night watch dating to 1631, constables older still, and no police. That improvised rescue is where the story of American police begins: not with crime, but with crowds.

Historian Alex Elkins argues that American rioting has adhered to three durable traditions: crowds acted to regulate communal morality, to defend the community against outside threats, and to protest government abuse of power. Rioters were rarely random criminals; they were neighbors and workmates who believed — often with broad public sympathy — that they were enforcing a rough popular justice the authorities would not. Rioting, Elkins emphasizes, "retained strong legitimacy and popular resonance" from 1800 into the 1960s, adapting English traditions of restrained, ritualized mobbing to more volatile urban conditions. Crucially, the state — especially the municipal police, once they existed — played a central role in "facilitating, constraining, or motivating unrest." Riots and police grew up together, each shaping the other.

The antebellum wave was staggering. Jacksonian cities burned with anti-abolitionist mobs (New York in 1834, the Garrison mob in 1835), anti-Catholic and nativist violence (the Ursuline convent burned in 1834; Philadelphia's Bible Riots of 1844 fought with muskets and even cannon), and attacks on Black neighborhoods — Philadelphia's abolitionist Pennsylvania Hall burned in 1838, three days after it opened. In the 1840s and 1850s, Elkins notes, white crowds fused the disorderly mob with the lynch mob, a lethal instrument of white settler sovereignty east and west: the riot was becoming less a ritual of protest than a technology of racial domination.

The deadliest urban uprising in American history combined all three traditions and turned them against the most vulnerable. In July 1863, after officials began drawing names under the new conscription law — which let the wealthy buy exemption for $300 — crowds of mostly Irish immigrant workers in New York attacked draft offices, Republican newspapers, and the homes of the rich. Within hours the protest curdled into a racial pogrom: over four days, rioters lynched Black New Yorkers and burned the Colored Orphan Asylum on Fifth Avenue. At least 119 people died before Union regiments, marched straight from Gettysburg, put down the rising. The city's new police fought the rioters, sometimes heroically; they could not stop them. Urban elites drew a lasting lesson: the city needed a standing force strong enough to hold the streets.

The racial pogrom reached its terrible maturity in the twentieth century. During the "Red Summer" of 1919, white mobs attacked Black communities in more than two dozen cities. In Chicago, the drowning of Eugene Williams, a Black teenager who had drifted across an invisible color line at a Lake Michigan beach — and a white policeman's refusal to arrest the stone-thrower witnesses blamed — ignited a week of violence that killed 38 people, 23 of them Black. Two years later in Tulsa, a white mob, some of its members deputized and armed by city officials, destroyed the prosperous Black district of Greenwood — "Black Wall Street" — burning some thirty-five square blocks and killing an estimated one to three hundred people. In both cases police stood aside or joined in. The pattern fits Elkins's periodization: from the 1860s to the 1930s, white communities used racial pogroms to seize power and enforce Jim Crow; from the 1910s to the 1970s, African Americans and Latinos increasingly rioted defensively against civilian and police violence — a lineage running from 1919 through Harlem to Watts, Newark, and Detroit.

Key Concept: Communal Policing

Before salaried police, communities policed themselves — through the watch, the posse, and, frequently, the mob. Professional police did not simply replace this tradition; they absorbed and sometimes deputized it — one reason the boundary between police and mob stayed blurry deep into the twentieth century.

From Watch to Force: Inventing the Police

For its first two centuries, urban America made do with a medieval English inheritance: the constable, an officer of the court, and the night watch, staffed by citizens taking turns — or, more often, by the poor and elderly men whom wealthier citizens paid to take their turns. The watchman walked his ward, called the hour, watched for fire, and raised a "hue and cry" if trouble came. Nobody expected him to investigate crime; a robbery victim identified the culprit himself and hired a constable, for a fee, to make the arrest. The word "police" was itself suspect — it sounded French, and despotic.

Two developments broke the old system. The first was London: in 1829, Home Secretary Robert Peel — after years of labor unrest at home and experience suppressing rebellion in Ireland — persuaded Parliament to create the Metropolitan Police, roughly three thousand salaried men under civilian commissioners, organized in military-style ranks, uniformed in blue with numbered badges and batons. Londoners called them "bobbies." The Met gave America a template: full-time, preventive, hierarchical patrol.

The second development was homegrown, and older. In the slave South, the essential policing institution was the slave patrol — mounted, armed bands of white men, mustered like the militia, empowered to stop any Black person, demand a pass, search dwellings, and administer whippings on the spot. South Carolina authorized its first patrol in 1702; Virginia followed in 1726, North Carolina in 1753. Charleston and New Orleans maintained paid, uniformed, armed City Guards decades before Boston or New York had anything comparable. In her 2020 New Yorker essay "The Invention of the Police," Jill Lepore places this lineage at the center of the story, insisting that any history beginning in London and Boston "ignores the role of slavery in the history of the police." Under the slave codes, she observes, surveillance and summary violence — "stop and frisk, stop and whip, shoot to kill" — were the ordinary instruments of governance.

The government of slavery was not a rule of law. It was a rule of police.

— Jill Lepore, The Invention of the Police, The New Yorker (2020)

Northern cities converted their watches into police departments in the two decades before the Civil War, under the pressure of exactly this disorder. Boston hired its first day police in 1838 and disbanded its ancient watch for a consolidated department in 1854 — the same year, Lepore notes pointedly, that the nativist Know-Nothings swept the city's elections. New York's unified Municipal Police, authorized in 1844, took to the streets in 1845; New Orleans, Cincinnati, Philadelphia, Chicago, and Baltimore followed in the 1850s. From the beginning, American departments diverged from Peel's model. They were decentralized and intensely local, run by political appointees, so that a change of party meant a change of police; a patrolman's job was often a machine reward; and, unlike the bobbies, they carried guns, at first their own, because the citizens they policed carried guns. In 1857 the politicization reached its absurd extreme: New York briefly had two rival forces, the mayor's Municipals and the state's Metropolitans, which fought a pitched battle on the steps of City Hall.

What did the new police actually do? Far less crime-fighting than legend suggests. Early departments swept drunks from the streets, returned lost children, enforced Sunday closing laws, lodged the homeless in station-house basements, broke strikes, and managed the friction of crowded neighborhoods. They were order-maintenance institutions — general-purpose regulators of urban life whose most consequential decisions were made by patrolmen on street corners. That became their enduring dilemma.

Reform came under the banner of "professionalization." Blue-ribbon investigations — most famously New York's Lexow Committee of 1894–1895, which documented a department run on purchased promotions and organized graft — pushed to take the police "out of politics." The movement found its prophet in August Vollmer, chief in Berkeley, California, from 1909, who introduced patrol cars, radio dispatch, crime laboratories, and college-educated recruits, and whose students spread the gospel nationwide. But Lepore complicates the heroic account. Vollmer was a veteran of the Philippine campaign of 1898 who understood policing in explicitly martial terms — "we're conducting a war," he said, "a war against the enemies of society" — and in Progressive-era practice those enemies included strikers, radicals, immigrants, and Black migrants, arrested at rates wildly disproportionate to their numbers. Policing Vice: Temperance and Prohibition

Nothing entangled the new police in the moral politics of the city more thoroughly than alcohol. The temperance and prohibition movement, as the historian H. Paul Thompson has argued, was arguably the longest-running reform movement in American history, stretching from the 1780s to repeal in 1933. It began as evangelical persuasion — the American Temperance Society (1826) and the Washingtonians of the 1840s asked individuals to take the pledge, a strategy called moral suasion. By mid-century, frustrated reformers turned to legal suasion: government coercion against the "liquor traffic" itself. Maine enacted the first statewide prohibition law in 1851, and a dozen states briefly followed; when state and local laws proved leaky, reformers went to Washington.

Two organizations carried the cause into the industrial city. The Woman's Christian Temperance Union, founded in 1874 and led after 1879 by Frances Willard, made the saloon a women's issue — where wages disappeared and domestic violence was primed — and under Willard's "Do Everything" policy linked temperance to suffrage and labor reform. The Anti-Saloon League, founded in Ohio in 1893, pioneered single-issue pressure politics, endorsing any candidate who would vote dry. Its campaign culminated in the Eighteenth Amendment, ratified in January 1919 and effective in 1920 under the Volstead Act, prohibiting the manufacture, sale, and transportation of intoxicating liquors nationwide.

Prohibition was urban politics by constitutional means. The saloon reformers hated was a specifically urban, immigrant institution — the workingman's club, the beer garden, the ward boss's headquarters, often the only public space in a tenement district with a toilet, a newspaper, and a free lunch. The dry coalition was disproportionately native-born, Protestant, and small-town; the wet opposition urban, Catholic and Jewish, and immigrant. "Wet" and "dry" became shorthand for the era's deepest cultural cleavage, running straight through the 1928 campaign of Al Smith, the wet Catholic governor of New York. For the police, Prohibition was a catastrophe: charged with enforcing a law millions of citizens — and many officers — regarded as illegitimate, departments were saturated with bootleggers' money on a scale that made the old saloon graft look quaint, while enforcement fell hardest on poor and immigrant neighborhoods. The triumph unraveled fast: liquor-fueled violence, organized opposition, and the Depression converged, and in December 1933 the Twenty-first Amendment repealed the Eighteenth — the only constitutional amendment ever undone.

Key Concept: Moral Suasion vs. Legal Suasion

Temperance reformers distinguished persuading individuals to abstain (moral suasion) from prohibiting the traffic by law (legal suasion). The shift, begun in the 1840s and completed in 1919, turned a religious reform movement into a policing project — and made every patrolman an agent of one side in a cultural war over how city people were allowed to live.

The Sex Trade and Its Regulators

Alcohol was one pillar of urban "vice"; commercial sex was the other. As the historian Jessica Pliley has shown, prostitution became a prominent feature of American cities in the early nineteenth century, sustained by young, unattached male migrants and by a labor market that paid women wages too low to live on. For some women, selling sex was a survival strategy; for a few, a route to economic independence; for all, it carried risks of disease, violence, and — significantly — harassment by the very police charged with regulating it. By mid-century, most cities had settled on an informal bargain: prostitution remained illegal but was tolerated within red-light districts, where brothels operated openly and paid regular tribute to police and politicians. The most famous, New Orleans's Storyville (1897–1917), was created by ordinance precisely to confine the trade to one district.

The Progressive Era destroyed this equilibrium. Vice commissions in dozens of cities — Chicago's 1911 report was the model — documented the districts and demanded abolition rather than regulation. A national panic over "white slavery," the belief that innocent white women were being coerced into brothels, produced the Mann Act of 1910, making it a federal crime to transport a woman across state lines for prostitution "or for any other immoral purpose" — elastic language soon stretched to police ordinary, and conspicuously interracial, consensual relationships. The decisive blow came with the First World War, when the federal government, anxious about recruits' venereal health, forced the closure of districts near training camps; Storyville was shut at the Navy's insistence in 1917. Pliley's key insight is that suppression did not end prostitution — it restructured it. Driven from semi-public brothels into clandestine streetwalking and call systems, sex workers lost the district's modest protections, becoming more dependent on pimps and more exposed to arrest. As with liquor, criminalizing vice enlarged police power while leaving the market intact.

The Patrolman's Dilemma: McAdoo to Wilson

By the early twentieth century, insiders already understood that something was structurally wrong. William McAdoo — New York City's police commissioner in 1904–1906, who then published his diagnosis of why the police were not more effective — offered one of the era's most candid accounts. The problem was not bad men but an impossible mandate. The commissioner was a short-term political appointee, unable to outlast the department's own internal "public opinion," which could quietly defeat any reform. The laws the police were told to enforce — Sunday closing, excise, gambling, prostitution — were laws large parts of the city did not accept, so enforcement inevitably became selective, and selective enforcement meant graft: every unenforceable moral statute was a license printed for blackmail. And the officer was asked to be everything at once.

The individual policeman on his post is at once soldier, watchman, detective, lawyer, judge, public censor of morals, and general regulator of human conduct, from spitting on the sidewalk to committing homicide, from the capturing of unlicensed dogs to giving builders and general contractors the free use of the public streets.

— William McAdoo, Guarding a Great City (1906)

Sixty-three years later, in the March 1969 Atlantic — an issue devoted to the police, in the shadow of the 1960s uprisings — the political scientist James Q. Wilson gave McAdoo's intuition its classic analytical form. Wilson's study of patrol work showed that the vast majority of what patrolmen actually did involved services and managing disorder — quarreling families, public drunks, bothersome teenagers, tavern fights — while only a small fraction involved law enforcement proper. The two functions are fundamentally different: law enforcement compares behavior to a clear legal standard, but order maintenance requires the officer to decide, on the spot, what acceptable behavior is — and public peace, unlike murder or theft, is defined by no statute.

Order maintenance arises out of a dispute among citizens who accuse each other of being at fault; law enforcement arises out of the victimization of an innocent party by a person whose guilt must be proved. Handling a disorderly situation requires the officer to make a judgment about what constitutes an appropriate standard of behavior; law enforcement requires him only to compare a person’s behavior with a clear legal standard.

— James Q. Wilson, The Dilemma of the Urban Police, The Atlantic (1969)

The dilemma follows. Because most disorder involves no arrestable offense, the patrolman must resolve it by improvisation, with almost no legal guidance — "alone, unsupervised, with no policies to guide him," Wilson wrote, the officer must "administer justice" on the curbstone. Yet the reform tradition since the 1890s had been built for the other function. Professionalization defined the police as crime-fighters, judged them by crime rates they could not control (departments, judged by a standard they could not meet, simply lied about the statistics), and stripped away the service roles that had connected them to neighborhoods. Every department, Wilson concluded, is really "two organizations in one": crime-fighting is served by centralization, specialization, and strict integrity; order maintenance by decentralization, foot patrol, wide discretion, and neighborhood involvement — and strategies that strengthen one weaken the other. No prepackaged reform resolves the tension. Wilson's focus on disorder later fed the 1982 "broken windows" hypothesis he coauthored with George Kelling, which shaped — critics say deformed — three decades of aggressive misdemeanor policing: the essay is both a description of the dilemma and an artifact of it.

Key Concept: Discretion

The most important decisions in policing are made at the bottom of the hierarchy, by the officer on the street, in encounters no supervisor sees and no court reviews. Discretion makes order maintenance possible — and it is the channel through which every prejudice of the officer and the era flows directly into citizens' lives.

Policing and Race

Every thread of this chapter — the riot, the watch, the vice squad, the patrolman's discretion — is knotted around race, and an honest institutional history has to say so plainly. The slave patrol was a fully developed system of racialized surveillance: universal stop-and-search powers over Black people, summary punishment, legal immunity for killing those who resisted. Emancipation abolished the patrols; it did not abolish their function. Under the Black Codes and then Jim Crow, Southern police enforced a racial order in which vagrancy arrests fed convict labor systems, and Northern departments, as the Great Migration remade their cities, policed Black neighborhoods with a heavier hand. The historian Khalil Gibran Muhammad calls the result the "condemnation of blackness": disproportionate patrol produced disproportionate arrest, then disproportionate conviction, which social scientists read as proof that Black people were disproportionately criminal — a closed loop doing the ideological work once done by the slave codes.

Balance requires care on two fronts. First, the slave-patrol lineage is one root among several: Northern departments grew most directly out of the watch, the London model, and the riot crisis, and scholars debate how much causal weight the Southern institution should bear for, say, Chicago. Second, none of this erases what Wilson documented — that most police work has always been mundane service and peacekeeping, much of it demanded by Black communities that were simultaneously over-policed and under-protected. Lepore holds both truths together: the crisis in policing, she writes, is the culmination of "a thousand other failures" — of education, social services, public health, gun regulation, and economic development — and the institution includes many dedicated public servants. But the pattern is not subtle. Police joined or tolerated the pogroms of 1863, 1919, and 1921; they enforced vice laws selectively against Black districts; and the two faces of mid-century policing — the reassuring television procedural, the fire hoses on the news from Birmingham and Selma — were one institution seen from two sides of the color line. Nearly every uprising of the 1960s began with a police encounter; the Kerner Commission's finding that they were rooted in white racism, ghetto conditions, and abusive policing was, in Elkins's terms, a rediscovery of the oldest riot tradition — the crowd protesting the government's abuse of power, with the police now the government's most visible face.

Health, Welfare, and Schools: The City Takes Care

How epidemics, filthy water, unschooled children, and desperate poverty forced nineteenth-century American cities to invent the institutions of collective care—and how each institution sorted the very people it promised to serve.

  • Describe the shift from miasma theory to germ theory, and account for the paradox that sanitary reform worked even when its science was wrong.
  • Apply Joel Tarr's "search for the ultimate sink" to explain why water supply without sewage treatment moved health problems downstream rather than solving them.

  • Explain the origins and doctrine of the first juvenile court (Cook County, 1899) and assess David Tanenhaus's account of its promise and problems.

The Summer the City Emptied

In June 1832, New Yorkers learned that Asiatic cholera—a disease that had marched out of India across Russia and Europe—had crossed the Atlantic. Within weeks it was in Manhattan. The symptoms were gruesome and swift: violent vomiting and diarrhea, skin turned blue and cold, death often within a day of the first cramp. No one knew what caused it. What New Yorkers did next tells us a great deal about the antebellum city: those who could afford to simply left. Tens of thousands—by some estimates a third or more of a population of roughly 250,000—fled to the countryside, leaving the disease to burn through the crowded wards of the poor, especially Irish immigrants and Black New Yorkers packed into cellars near the docks. More than 3,500 died that summer. Many respectable observers drew a comfortable conclusion: cholera was a scourge of the dirty, the drunken, and the vicious—a judgment on individual sin rather than a failure of the city itself.

That interpretation could not survive the century. Cholera returned in 1849 and 1866; yellow fever devastated the urban South. Each epidemic posed the same question with mounting urgency: was a city's death rate an act of God, or an artifact of its water, wastes, housing, and government? Between the 1840s and the 1920s, American cities answered by building something genuinely new: a public sector of care. This chapter follows five of its branches—public health (Daniel Wilson), water and sewers (Joel Tarr), schools (Ansley Erickson), juvenile justice (David Tanenhaus), and poor relief (Michael Katz). A common thread runs through all five: each institution promised universal benevolence, and each sorted the people it served—into clean and dirty, deserving and undeserving, redeemable and incorrigible. The city learned to take care; it never stopped taking judgment.

The Deadly City

The nineteenth-century American city was, by our standards, astonishingly lethal. As Wilson emphasizes, rapid urbanization outran every sanitary arrangement inherited from the town: privies overflowed into drinking wells, pigs scavenged garbage in the streets, milk arrived unrefrigerated and adulterated. Tuberculosis, typhoid, diphtheria, and scarlet fever killed steadily; infants died in appalling numbers each summer. For much of the century, big-city death rates so exceeded rural ones that urban populations grew only because migrants kept arriving.

Against this endemic background, epidemics arrived as shocks—and shocks forced political change. In 1832 the dominant response to cholera was flight, prayer, and moralizing. By 1849, when the disease killed more than 5,000 New Yorkers and thousands more in St. Louis, Cincinnati, and New Orleans, some observers noticed that cholera tracked water sources and concentrations of filth more faithfully than it tracked sin—the insight the London physician John Snow demonstrated in 1854 by tracing a Soho outbreak to the Broad Street pump. By 1866, New York was ready to act. With cholera approaching, the state legislature that February created the Metropolitan Board of Health—the first permanent, professionally staffed municipal health authority in the United States with real legal power. When the epidemic arrived, the board responded with organization instead of panic: mapping cases, disinfecting premises, clearing filth. The city's cholera deaths came to roughly a tenth of the 1849 toll. The lesson was unmistakable: government action could measurably change a city's death rate.

The urban South learned the same lesson in a harder school. In 1878, yellow fever—mosquito-borne, though no one yet knew it—swept up the Mississippi Valley. In Memphis, where some 25,000 residents fled, more than 5,000 of those remaining died. The catastrophe bankrupted the city, which surrendered its charter and was governed for years as a state taxing district. Out of the wreckage came a sanitary revolution: a house-by-house sanitary survey, and in the early 1880s a new sewerage system on the small-pipe plan of engineer George Waring that became a national model. The epidemic also spurred the short-lived National Board of Health (1879)—an early acknowledgment that disease ignored municipal boundaries.

Key Concept: Epidemics as Political Events

Historians treat epidemics as stress tests that expose a society's structure: who flees and who cannot, who is blamed, and what institutions get built afterward. Cholera in 1832 produced moral condemnation of the poor; cholera in 1866 produced the Metropolitan Board of Health; yellow fever in 1878 produced municipal bankruptcy and a model sewer system. The pattern—crisis, blame, then institution-building—recurs throughout this chapter.

Water In, Waste Out: Tarr and the Search for the Ultimate Sink

Behind the epidemics lay a problem that Joel Tarr, the leading historian of urban infrastructure, frames as "the search for the ultimate sink": every city must put its wastes somewhere—into the air, the water, or the land—and each generation's solution tends to become the next generation's crisis, as pollution is displaced rather than eliminated, from one medium, one place, or one population to another.

Consider the water supply. Early cities drew on local wells and cisterns, supplemented by private companies of unreliable quality—New York's Manhattan Company, chartered in 1799, cared more about the banking privileges buried in its charter than about clean water. As cities grew, local sources became inadequate and dangerously contaminated by the privy vaults honeycombing urban land. The solution was the great public waterworks: Philadelphia's pioneering Fairmount Works, and above all New York's Croton Aqueduct, completed in 1842, carrying water by gravity forty-one miles from Westchester County into Manhattan. The Croton celebration that October—fountains leaping in City Hall Park, a five-mile parade—marked a real achievement: abundant water for drinking, washing, and firefighting, and proof that a municipal government could plan, finance, and build on a monumental scale. By century's end most sizable cities owned their waterworks, among the first great expansions of municipal enterprise.

But here Tarr's framework bites. Piped-in water meant vastly more water used—and, once households installed the water closet, vastly more contaminated water to dispose of. Cesspools designed for a dry-waste world overflowed. Cities built sewers—Chicago, under engineer Ellis Chesbrough in the late 1850s, built the nation's first comprehensive system, famously raising the grade of its streets to make gravity drainage possible—and adopted "water-carriage" disposal, flushing waste into the nearest river or lake. The sanitary gain inside the city was immediate. So was the catch: the nearest river was usually some other city's drinking water. Typhoid death rates climbed in downstream cities; Pittsburgh, drawing on rivers that served as upstream sewers, suffered some of the worst typhoid mortality in the nation. The sink was never ultimate. The waste always surfaced somewhere else.

Chicago provided the era's most audacious illustration. The city drank from Lake Michigan and discharged sewage into the Chicago River, which flowed into Lake Michigan—a closed loop of self-poisoning. Rather than treat its sewage, Chicago built the Sanitary and Ship Canal, opened in January 1900, reversing the river's flow so that the city's wastes ran away from the lake, down the Illinois valley toward the Mississippi. It was the search for the ultimate sink made literal—Chicago's health purchased, as St. Louis argued in a lawsuit that reached the Supreme Court, at the expense of everyone downstream. The durable fix came from treatment: after about 1908, filtration and chlorination of drinking water spread rapidly, and urban typhoid death rates collapsed—one of the great public health victories in American history. Characteristically, cities found it cheaper to purify the water they took in than the sewage they sent out; serious sewage treatment lagged for decades.

Key Concept: The Search for the Ultimate Sink

Tarr's phrase for the recurring pattern of urban environmental history: wastes are never destroyed, only relocated—from land to water, from water to land or air, from one city to the cities downstream, from richer neighborhoods to poorer ones. The framework teaches us to ask of any sanitary improvement: where did the problem go, and who lives there?

From Miasma to Microbe

The sanitary systems of mid-century were built on a theory of disease that turned out to be wrong—one of the most instructive paradoxes in urban history. The reigning doctrine was miasma (or "filth") theory: epidemic disease arose from foul emanations given off by decaying organic matter. Disease, on this view, was literally in the air, and the smell of a neighborhood was a fair index of its danger.

Miasma theory had energetic champions. In England, Edwin Chadwick—a lawyer, not a physician, and architect of the harsh New Poor Law of 1834—produced the Report on the Sanitary Condition of the Labouring Population of Great Britain (1842), which correlated filth, overcrowding, and early death and launched the international sanitary movement. His chief American counterpart, the Quaker physician John H. Griscom, published The Sanitary Condition of the Laboring Population of New York in 1845, documenting the cellar dwellings and lightless courts in which the city's poor lived and died. Of the thousands living in cellars, Griscom was unsparing: such holes, he wrote, "are properly adapted only as receptacles for the dead." And he insisted on a claim that was, in its day, radical—that this suffering was preventable, being in a great degree the result of "causes which are removeable." Sanitary reform, he argued, was

a measure of humanity, of justice to the poor, of safety to the whole people, and of economy to the public treasury.

— John H. Griscom, The Sanitary Condition of the Laboring Population of New York (1845)

Griscom's fourfold appeal—compassion, justice, self-protection, and thrift—became the standard formula of American sanitary reform, echoed in Lemuel Shattuck's Massachusetts report of 1850 and in the Citizens' Association survey that helped win New York's health board in 1866. Note what the formula did politically: it recast the health of the poor as the business of the whole city. Disease bred in the Five Points did not stay there; it traveled uptown with the laundress and the milk can. Fear, as much as sympathy, built the sanitary city.

Miasma theory was mistaken about mechanism—smells do not cause cholera—but its prescriptions were often fortuitously right: cleaning streets, emptying privies, and above all separating drinking water from human waste attacked the actual pathways of disease. Then the science caught up. Louis Pasteur and Robert Koch established that specific microbes cause specific diseases—Koch identified the tuberculosis bacillus in 1882 and the cholera vibrio in 1883—and by the 1890s cities were founding bacteriological laboratories; New York's became world-famous for its diphtheria antitoxin work. Germ theory turned public health from a general assault on filth into precise interventions: filtration and chlorination, milk pasteurization, antitoxin and immunization, case reporting, and the tracking of healthy carriers—most notoriously Mary Mallon, "Typhoid Mary," an immigrant cook detained by New York health authorities for years without trial. Wilson notes the double edge: the new public health saved lives on a vast scale, but its coercive powers—quarantine, detention, compulsory vaccination upheld in Jacobson v. Massachusetts (1905)—fell heaviest on immigrants and the poor, who were also the people most often blamed for disease in the first place.

Schooling the City's Children

If the sanitary movement addressed the city's body, the common school movement addressed what reformers saw as its soul. Ansley Erickson's history of schools in U.S. cities makes a point easy to miss today: public schooling was not a natural feature of American life but an urban invention, assembled in the same decades—and often in response to the same anxieties—as the waterworks and the board of health. City schools, she emphasizes, have always been asked to serve both children and the city's ambitions for what its people should become; every urban conflict—over immigration, religion, race, labor—eventually shows up at the schoolhouse door.

The founding argument was Horace Mann's. As secretary of the Massachusetts Board of Education from 1837 to 1848, Mann campaigned for tax-supported, nonsectarian "common schools" mixing the children of all classes. His Twelfth Annual Report (1848) gave the movement its most famous sentence:

Education, then, beyond all other devices of human origin, is the great equalizer of the conditions of men,—the balance-wheel of the social machinery.

— Horace Mann, Twelfth Annual Report to the Massachusetts Board of Education (1848)

Mann wrote against the backdrop of famine immigration, widening inequality, and Protestant alarm at both. The common school promised to prevent class war and to manufacture citizens from the children of strangers. Cities took up the project first because they had the tax base, the density, and the perceived problem: masses of poor, foreign, Catholic children in the streets. The promise was universal; the curriculum, saturated with Protestant Bible-reading, was not. New York's school wars of the early 1840s—in which Bishop John Hughes demanded public funds for Catholic schools and was refused—fixed the pattern for a century: officially neutral public schools that Catholics experienced as sectarian, answered by a vast parallel parochial system.

Over the second half of the century, urban schooling was transformed from a scatter of ward-run schoolhouses into the age-graded, superintendent-led bureaucracy we still recognize. The change had a logic—graded classrooms, uniform curricula, certified teachers—and a politics: Progressive-era reformers, drawn largely from business elites, abolished the ward school boards through which immigrant communities had exercised real influence, in favor of small citywide boards they could dominate. Centralization was sold as taking schools "out of politics"; in practice it moved politics upward, away from the neighborhoods. Meanwhile the state's claim on children hardened: Massachusetts enacted the first compulsory attendance law in 1852, and by 1918 every state had one, working in tandem with child labor laws to move children from mill and street to classroom.

Two features of the mature system deserve attention. The first is Americanization: in the peak immigration decades, schools took on the explicit mission of turning immigrant children into English-speaking Americans, by means ranging from the generous (kindergartens, school baths, hot lunches, evening classes) to the coercive (punishment for speaking other languages, curricula teaching children to be ashamed of their parents). The second is stratification: the era that universalized schooling also invented tracking—IQ testing and "differentiated" vocational curricula sorted children, disproportionately by class, ethnicity, and race, into different destinies. And as the Great Migration brought Black southerners to northern cities, school boundaries, transfers, and teacher assignments quietly produced separate and unequal schools without a single statute requiring them. Erickson's central irony stands: the institution founded as "the great equalizer" became one of the city's most effective engines for reproducing inequality—which is why it later became a central battleground of the civil rights movement.

Inventing the Juvenile Court

On July 1, 1899, the Cook County Juvenile Court opened in Chicago—the first juvenile court in the world. Its creation, the subject of David Tanenhaus's history, was a characteristic Progressive-era achievement: a coalition of women reformers, settlement workers, and lawyers persuaded the Illinois legislature that the city's children required a separate justice system. The Chicago Woman's Club and the residents of Jane Addams's Hull House—including Julia Lathrop, later first head of the federal Children's Bureau—had documented what everyone could see: children as young as eight were confined in police lockups and the county jail alongside adult criminals, tried in the same courts, and stamped with criminal records for offenses ranging from theft to simply being poor and unsupervised in the streets.

The Illinois Juvenile Court Act of 1899 rested on the equity doctrine of parens patriae—the state as parent. A child who stole or ran wild was not a criminal to be punished but a ward to be saved; the question was not "what did this child do?" but "what does this child need?" From that premise flowed machinery invented in Chicago and copied everywhere: informal hearings without juries or lawyers; confidential records to spare lifelong stigma; probation officers—at first volunteers, many of them women—who supervised children in their own neighborhoods rather than committing them to institutions; and jurisdiction over "dependent" and "neglected" children as well as "delinquent" ones, erasing the line between the child who broke the law and the child whom poverty had broken. Judge Julian Mack, who presided in the court's formative years, said the child should be treated as "a wise and merciful father handles his own child." The idea spread with remarkable speed: within a decade most states had juvenile courts, and by 1925 all but two did.

Tanenhaus complicates both the celebratory and the cynical readings. The court was a running improvisation—starved of funds, dependent on philanthropy, and challenged by parents who, not unreasonably, saw parens patriae as a license to seize their children without due process. Its clientele was overwhelmingly the children of the immigrant poor, and its judgments about "neglect" and "immorality"—especially for girls, brought in largely for sexual precocity rather than crime—enforced middle-class Protestant norms on Catholic, Jewish, and Black migrant families. Because the court claimed to help rather than punish, it operated free of procedural protections: a child could be committed to a reformatory until twenty-one on evidence that would never have convicted an adult. That paradox—unchecked discretion justified by good intentions—persisted until the Supreme Court's In re Gault decision (1967) extended due process to children. Yet Tanenhaus resists reducing the court to social control: it kept children out of jails, invented probation as an alternative to the reformatory, and institutionalized the radical idea that childhood is a legal status deserving distinct treatment—an idea, born in Chicago, that circled the globe.

Key Concept: Parens Patriae

Latin for "parent of the country": the doctrine that the state may act as guardian for those who cannot care for themselves. The juvenile court used it to intervene in children's lives without criminal charge, jury, or counsel—for their own good. Its double character, protective and coercive at once, recurs throughout this chapter: whenever the city takes care, it also takes control.

The Shadow of the Poorhouse: Katz and the Moral Sorting of Poverty

Beneath all these institutions lay the oldest urban question: what to do about the poor. Michael Katz's In the Shadow of the Poorhouse, the standard social history of American welfare, opens with a provocation: American welfare did not begin with the New Deal, and it was never primarily about relieving misery. From the colonial era forward, poor relief was a system for sorting the poor—deciding who deserved help, on what terms, and with how much humiliation attached.

The inherited system, adapted from the Elizabethan poor laws, made each locality responsible for its own paupers through "outdoor relief"—aid in cash, food, or fuel to people in their own homes—or through auctioning paupers to the lowest bidder. In the early nineteenth century, reformers turned decisively against outdoor relief. Influential investigations—the Quincy Report in Massachusetts (1821) and the Yates Report in New York (1824)—concluded that aid given at home rewarded idleness and manufactured paupers. Their solution was the poorhouse: an institution whose regime of work, discipline, and abstinence would deter the able-bodied from seeking relief while reforming the character of those who entered. In the same decades that cities built penitentiaries, asylums, and reform schools, counties across the nation built poorhouses—all reflecting, Katz argues, the same institutional faith: that designed environments could remake flawed people, and that poverty was at root a defect of character rather than a feature of the labor market.

The reality mocked the theory. Katz's verdict is unsparing:

Miserable, poorly managed, underfunded institutions, trapped by their own contradictions, poorhouses failed to meet any of the goals so confidently predicted by their sponsors.

— Michael B. Katz, In the Shadow of the Poorhouse: A Social History of Welfare in America (1986)

The contradictions were structural. A poorhouse was supposed to deter the lazy and shelter the helpless, punish and care, cut costs and reform character—goals that undermined one another. Make the house harsh enough to deter the able-bodied, and it brutalized the sick, old, and young who actually filled it; the able-bodied mostly stayed away, or used it strategically as a winter refuge between stints of seasonal work. In practice the poorhouse became a dumping ground for those with nowhere else to go—the aged, the chronically ill, the insane, unwed mothers, children—run on funds taxpayers begrudged, its work-discipline a fiction since few inmates could work. Over the following decades it was dismembered by exit: the insane to state asylums, children to orphanages and placing-out schemes, the sick to the municipal hospitals that grew out of almshouse infirmaries. What remained by 1900 was in effect a grim public old-age home, and the terror of "ending in the poorhouse" haunted working-class old age until Social Security. That terror was not entirely a bug: the poorhouse's most lasting function, Katz observes, was as a threat, disciplining the labor force by making dependence visibly worse than the lowest-paid work.

Outdoor relief never actually disappeared—it was cheaper and less cruel, and machine politicians (recall Plunkitt in Chapter 4) ran their own informal welfare beside it—but it came under renewed assault in the Gilded Age, when major cities including Brooklyn and Philadelphia abolished public outdoor relief outright. The intellectual force behind the assault was "scientific charity," embodied in the Charity Organization Societies that spread from Buffalo (1877) across urban America. The COS creed held that indiscriminate almsgiving manufactured paupers; that charity must be centralized and investigated; and that the poor needed "not alms but a friend"—the friendly visitor, usually a woman of the comfortable classes, who would inspect each applicant's home and character and supply moral uplift in place of money. Katz treats scientific charity as the purest expression of the ideology running through the whole system: the division of the poor into the deserving—widows, orphans, the sick and aged, blameless victims of misfortune—and the undeserving—the able-bodied and "shiftless," whose need was read as moral failure. The categories were administratively convenient and morally comforting, but they never described reality: they ignored the actual causes of urban poverty—low and seasonal wages, industrial accidents, sickness, old age in a wage economy—and they endure in American debates that still sort "working families" from "welfare dependents." There is a final irony: casework taught many friendly visitors that character was not the problem, and scientific charity thereby incubated professional social work and a generation of reformers who pushed structural remedies—mothers' pensions (Illinois, 1911), workmen's compensation, and ultimately the Social Security Act of 1935, itself carefully split between honorable "insurance" for workers and stigmatized "relief" for the rest. The poorhouse was gone; its shadow, Katz's title reminds us, was not.

The City That Learned to Care—Selectively

Between 1840 and 1930 the American city built a public sector of care unimaginable to the generation that fled cholera in 1832: aqueducts and sewers, health departments and laboratories, school systems enrolling nearly every child, courts designed around childhood, and a welfare apparatus from poorhouse to mothers' pension. Mortality fell dramatically; the city ceased to be a demographic sinkhole. This was one of the great achievements of American government—accomplished mostly by cities and states, decades before Washington entered the field. Yet every one of these institutions embedded a sorting principle. Public health protected the whole city but policed the immigrant and the poor; the sanitary state sent its wastes downstream. The common school equalized and stratified in the same gesture. The juvenile court rescued children by suspending their rights. Poor relief priced aid in humiliation. The question these institutions answered was never simply "how shall the city care for its people?" but always also "which people, on whose terms, and at whose expense?"

Mass Transit: Moving the American City

How a century of omnibuses, horsecars, cable lines, trolleys, elevateds, and subways stretched the American city outward, concentrated its business at the center, and then—caught between fixed fares, franchise politics, and the automobile—collapsed as a private industry.

Learning Objectives

  • Trace the technological succession of urban transit—omnibus, horsecar, cable car, electric streetcar, elevated railway, and subway—and explain what problem each innovation solved and what new problems it created.
  • Explain, following Sam Bass Warner Jr., how streetcar lines produced the "streetcar suburb" and sorted the metropolis by class and distance.
  • Analyze, following Robert Fogelson, how mass transit first built the downtown business district and then helped undermine it.
  • Evaluate the politics of transit as a private business operating under public franchise: traction magnates, municipal corruption, the five-cent fare, and the eventual public takeover.
  • Assess competing explanations for the decline of mass transit after World War II, including the contested "General Motors streetcar conspiracy."

Thursday, October 27, 1904, was a civic holiday in New York in everything but name. At one o'clock, dignitaries gathered at City Hall for speeches; then they descended into a jewel-box of a station beneath City Hall Park—vaulted in white Guastavino tile edged with green and brown, lit by leaded-glass skylights and brass chandeliers. At 2:34 p.m., Mayor George B. McClellan Jr. grasped a silver controller handle made for the occasion and started the first official train of the Interborough Rapid Transit Company north beneath the streets of Manhattan. The mayor, who was supposed to hand the controls to a professional motorman, enjoyed himself so much that he kept driving for miles. The president of the Rapid Transit Board had asked New Yorkers to mark the moment by blowing whistles and ringing bells, and the city obliged.

That evening the subway opened to the public at a nickel a ride. Some 27,000 invited guests had ridden free during the afternoon; roughly 125,000 more paid to ride in the six hours after 7 p.m., packing cars behind express trains that averaged around thirty miles an hour—a speed no streetcar stuck behind wagons and pushcarts could dream of. The next morning's newspapers caught the mood in their stacked headlines, tallying the crowds and marveling at the mayor's turn as motorman.

The opening of the IRT was the crescendo of an eighty-year transformation. In 1820, no American city had any public transportation at all; everyone who could not afford a private carriage walked. By 1904, the largest American cities had built layer upon layer of transit—horse-drawn, cable-hauled, electric, elevated, and underground—and those systems had remade urban space itself: stretching cities miles beyond their walking-era boundaries, sorting residents by class along radial corridors, and funneling hundreds of thousands of riders each day into a single dense district that Americans learned to call "downtown." This chapter follows that transformation, guided especially by the historian Robert M. Fogelson—longtime MIT professor of urban studies and history—whose book Downtown: Its Rise and Fall, 1880–1950 (2001) argues that mass transit was both the making and, eventually, the unmaking of the American central business district.

Before the Machine: The Walking City and the Omnibus

The American city of the early nineteenth century was a walking city. It was compact of necessity—rarely more than two miles across—because the only universal means of getting around was on foot. Rich and poor lived close together and close to work; merchants often lived above or beside their counting houses. There was no "commute," and no real separation between the districts where people worked and lived. The sorting of the metropolis into specialized zones—a business core, industrial belts, residential rings graded by income—awaited a technology that could move ordinary people faster than their legs.

The first such technology was modest: the omnibus, a large horse-drawn coach running a fixed route on a schedule, picking up anyone who could pay. Pioneered in France in the 1820s, omnibuses appeared on the streets of New York by the end of that decade and soon spread to Philadelphia, Boston, and other cities. The omnibus was an institutional innovation more than a mechanical one—the idea that a vehicle could be public, open to strangers sharing a ride for a fare—but as transportation it left much to be desired. It was slow, jolting over cobblestones or wallowing in mud, crowded, and relatively expensive; fares put daily use beyond the reach of laborers. Contemporary riders complained constantly of the crush and the discomfort. Still, the omnibus established the pattern that every later mode would elaborate: fixed routes radiating from the center of the city, carrying fare-paying passengers between home and work.

The Horsecar City

The next step was to put the horse-drawn car on rails. The New York and Harlem Railroad began running horsecars in Manhattan in 1832, and New Orleans acquired street railways in the same era. The improvement sounds trivial—iron wheels on iron rails—but it was decisive: the low rolling resistance of rail let a horse pull far more weight than it could haul through the muck of an unpaved street, and it gave passengers a smoother, faster, cheaper ride. From the 1850s onward, the horsecar spread rapidly; by 1860 New York, Brooklyn, Boston, Philadelphia, Baltimore, Pittsburgh, Chicago, Cincinnati, and New Orleans all had networks. By the mid-1880s the United States had more than 400 street railway companies operating over 6,000 miles of track and carrying on the order of 188 million passengers a year. By 1890, New Yorkers were averaging nearly 300 horsecar rides per person per year.

The horsecar began the physical stretching of the city. With service at five or six miles an hour, the practical radius of settlement roughly doubled, and the first ring of "horsecar suburbs" grew up along the lines. It also began the social sorting: families who could afford the daily fare could now live at a distance from workshops, wharves, and slaughterhouses, while those who could not remained walking distance from work. The historian Kenneth Jackson and others have observed that with the horsecar, transportation began to separate the classes in space—an ominous novelty in cities that had always jumbled everyone together.

But the horsecar city ran on live animals, and the animals were its weakness. A big system needed ten or more horses for every car in service, since a horse could work only four or five hours a day; the work was so punishing that a streetcar horse lasted only a few years. Stables, feed, veterinary care, and the disposal of prodigious quantities of manure were enormous costs—and public nuisances. In the autumn of 1872, a great epizootic of equine influenza swept North American cities, sickening horses en masse and bringing street railway service to a crawl; the epidemic was a vivid demonstration of how completely urban life depended on horseflesh, and it sharpened the search for mechanical power.

Cables and Currents: Electricity and the Streetcar Suburb

The first serious mechanical alternative was the cable car. In 1873, Andrew Smith Hallidie's Clay Street Hill Railroad in San Francisco demonstrated that cars could be hauled by gripping an endless steel cable running in a slot beneath the street, driven by a stationary steam engine. Cable traction conquered hills no horse could manage, and it eliminated the stable. It spread well beyond hilly cities: the Chicago City Railway, opened in 1882, built the largest and most profitable cable system in the world, using grip cars to pull trains of trailers through flat, congested streets. But cable had crippling drawbacks. Construction was fantastically expensive, the machinery was temperamental, and the physics were absurd—the vast majority of the power generated went simply to dragging miles of heavy cable through its conduit, whether one car or a hundred was attached.

The real revolution came from electricity. After decades of failed experiments by dozens of inventors, a young engineer named Frank J. Sprague—a former assistant to Edison and a graduate of the Naval Academy—equipped the Richmond Union Passenger Railway in Virginia, which entered regular service on February 2, 1888. Sprague's system solved the practical problems that had defeated his predecessors: current collected from an overhead wire by a spring-loaded trolley pole, and motors mounted so they could drive the axles reliably over rough track, steep grades, and sharp curves. Richmond worked, day after day, and the industry noticed. A delegation from Boston's West End Street Railway—then the largest street railway company in the country—inspected the Richmond line in September 1888 and promptly committed to electrification. The stampede was on. By 1895 the United States had nearly 900 electric street railways operating roughly 11,000 miles of track; total street railway mileage exploded from about 5,800 miles in 1890 to more than 34,000 by 1907, by which time the horse had all but vanished from American transit.

The electric streetcar—the "trolley"—was the great city-builder of the age. It was twice as fast as a horsecar and far cheaper to operate, which allowed companies to lower effective fares, extend lines miles into open country, and offer free transfers that let a rider cross the metropolis for a nickel. Real estate speculators grasped the implications immediately; indeed, many trolley lines were built at a loss precisely to sell house lots along the route. The result was a new urban form: the streetcar suburb.

Key Concept: Warner's Streetcar Suburbs and the "Two-Part City"

In Streetcar Suburbs: The Process of Growth in Boston, 1870–1900 (1962), the historian Sam Bass Warner Jr.—who taught at MIT and Boston University—showed how the horsecar and then the electric streetcar transformed Boston's Roxbury, West Roxbury, and Dorchester from rural towns into a vast suburban belt. The agents of this transformation were not master planners but thousands of small builders and individual landowners, each making modest, similar decisions—a grid of streets here, a few three-deckers there—within the frame set by the streetcar lines and the market. The outcome was a metropolis divided into what Warner called a two-part city: an inner city of work and low-income residence, and an outer ring of home. Because access to the new suburbs was rationed by fare and by house price, the rings sorted themselves by income, with the comfortable middle class moving outward and the poor left behind—segregation achieved not by decree but by tens of thousands of private choices.

Fogelson's Downtown supplies the other half of the story. Every streetcar line ran downtown—that was the point. The radial geometry of transit concentrated shopping, offices, banking, entertainment, and government in a few square blocks at the convergence of the lines, creating by the 1880s the most intensely developed real estate in the world. The department store, the skyscraper, and the rush hour were all creatures of this convergence. Downtown, in Fogelson's account, was not a natural fact but a spatial artifact of the transit network—the place everyone could reach because every line went there—and its property owners would spend the next half-century fighting to keep the network pointed at their front doors.

Over and Under: Elevateds and Subways

The streetcar's success created its own crisis: congestion. Trolleys shared the street with wagons, carriages, pushcarts, and pedestrians, and in the biggest cities the converging lines simply jammed. The obvious answer was to separate transit from the street—to go over it or under it. Rapid transit, as the faster grade-separated systems were called, was staggeringly expensive, and the battles over who would build it, who would profit from it, and where it would run became, as Fogelson shows, central dramas of urban politics.

New York went up first. Charles Harvey's West Side and Yonkers Patent Railway, an elevated line along Greenwich Street and Ninth Avenue built between 1867 and 1870, began with cable haulage and switched to small steam locomotives; by the late 1870s the Manhattan Railway Company was running elevated trains on Second, Third, Sixth, and Ninth Avenues. The els moved crowds at unprecedented speed, but they were grim neighbors—dripping oil and cinders, darkening the streets beneath, rattling windows. Chicago followed with its first elevated line on the South Side in June 1892, and in 1897 the elevated Union Loop was completed around the central business district, giving Chicago's downtown its enduring name: the Loop.

Going underground took longer, partly because the technology was daunting and partly because the politics were worse. New York had actually possessed a tiny subway as early as 1870, when Alfred Ely Beach, editor of Scientific American, secretly dug a 300-foot pneumatic demonstration tunnel beneath Broadway in 58 days—having obtained a permit for a package-delivery tube because the Tammany machine would not grant him one for passengers. A giant fan blew a single elegant car back and forth; a quarter bought admission. The press was charmed.

The most novel, if not the most successful, enterprise that New-York has seen.

— The New York Times on Alfred Ely Beach's pneumatic subway (1870)

Beach's toy died in litigation and the Panic of 1873, and it was Boston, not New York, that opened America's first real subway. By the 1890s, Tremont Street along Boston Common was so choked with streetcars that pedestrians joked they could cross downtown by stepping from car roof to car roof. The solution—opposed by merchants fearing disruption and by citizens who found riding through the ground unnatural—was a short tunnel to take the streetcars off the surface. On September 1, 1897, the Tremont Street subway opened, the first in North America, and Bostonians crowded aboard.

First Car off the Earth: Allston Electric Goes into the Subway on Schedule Time.

— Boston Daily Globe (September 1, 1897)

Boston's subway was really a streetcar tunnel—short, shallow, and slow—but it proved that Americans would ride beneath the earth. New York's answer was of another order of magnitude. After decades of failed schemes, the city adopted a novel public-private formula: the city would finance construction, and a private contractor would build, equip, and operate the line under lease. Contract 1 was signed in February 1900 with John B. McDonald's construction company, bankrolled by the financier August Belmont Jr., who organized the Interborough Rapid Transit Company to run the system; ground was broken at City Hall that March. Chief engineer William Barclay Parsons, who had studied the subways of Europe, insisted on four tracks so express trains could overtake locals—the feature that made the New York subway genuinely rapid transit. Four and a half years later came the opening day with which this chapter began.

Our Subway Open: 150,000 Try It. M'Clellan Motorman of First Subway Train; Mayor Used Silver Controller to Start Official Trip.

— The New York Times headlines (October 28, 1904)

The subway did for the outer boroughs what the streetcar had done for the near suburbs: it threw open cheap land to dense settlement. Under the Dual Contracts of 1913, the city and the two private operators—the IRT and the Brooklyn Rapid Transit Company (BRT)—jointly financed a vast expansion that carried lines deep into the Bronx, Brooklyn, and Queens, and most of today's system was built or improved under those agreements. Farm fields sprouted apartment blocks within a decade of the trains' arrival. Mass transit was now shaping the metropolis at both ends: pumping ever-larger crowds into downtown by day and dispersing them ever farther outward by night.

Traction Politics: Franchises, Magnates, and the Five-Cent Fare

Every mile of this infrastructure ran on public streets under private ownership—and that arrangement made transit the most politically explosive business in urban America. A street railway needed a franchise, a grant from the city council permitting it to lay track in the public way. Franchises were worth fortunes, and city councils sold them—sometimes literally. The traction magnate, assembling monopolies out of competing lines and buying aldermen wholesale, became a stock villain of the age, and no one played the part more vividly than Charles Tyson Yerkes of Chicago.

Yerkes, a Philadelphia financier who had served prison time for misappropriating city funds before reinventing himself in Chicago in 1881, gained control of the North Chicago Street Railway in 1886 and went on to dominate the street railways of the city's North and West Sides, along with the elevated lines whose downtown Union Loop he drove to completion in 1897. He was candid about his methods—contemporaries reported his formula for traction finance as buying up old junk, fixing it up a little, and unloading it on other fellows—and unapologetic about crowding: a maxim widely attributed to him held that it was the straphangers, packed in and clinging to the overhead straps, who paid the dividends. He was, as his biographers concede, "not averse to using bribery and blackmail." He fell when he overreached: after Governor John Peter Altgeld vetoed his first attempt to secure long-term franchises from the state, Yerkes got the Allen Law through the legislature in 1897, allowing city councils to grant extended franchises. Chicago erupted. When the fight reached the city council in 1898, a partially reformed council and Mayor Carter Harrison IV—with swing votes from the notorious First Ward aldermen "Hinky Dink" Kenna and "Bathhouse" John Coughlin, who for once found honesty the better bargain—defeated him. Yerkes sold his Chicago holdings in 1899 and decamped to London, where he organized the company that electrified and expanded the London Underground. Theodore Dreiser immortalized him as Frank Cowperwood in The Financier and The Titan.

The Yerkes affair fed a broader Progressive-era conviction: that transit was not an ordinary business but a public utility, affected with a public interest, requiring regulation if not outright municipal ownership. The most consequential instrument of that conviction was the fare. In New York, the city's contracts with the IRT—and later the Dual Contracts with both companies—fixed the fare at five cents. The nickel fare was wildly popular, a guarantee that the workingman's ride would stay cheap, and no politician dared touch it. But it was fixed in nominal terms, and the wartime inflation after 1917 roughly halved its real value while wages and coal prices soared. The companies were squeezed toward insolvency; the BRT, reeling also from the horrific Malbone Street wreck of November 1918, went bankrupt that year and re-emerged as the BMT in 1923. Mayor John Hylan, who built his career on the five-cent fare and hatred of the "traction interests," blocked every relief measure and pushed the city to build its own competing system—the municipally owned and operated Independent Subway (IND), opened beginning in 1932.

Key Concept: The Five-Cent Fare and the Fate of Private Transit

The nickel fare illustrates a central dilemma of transit as a regulated private business. The fare was a political price, not an economic one: fixed by contract and defended by voters, it could not rise with costs. Inflation after World War I turned a profitable rate into a ruinous one, starving the companies of capital for maintenance and expansion even as ridership grew. Unable to raise prices, unable to abandon service, the private operators slid toward bankruptcy—and the public, which had come to depend on the systems, had little choice but to take them over. In New York the fare did not rise until 1947 (to a dime; fifteen cents followed in 1953)—seven years after the companies themselves had ceased to exist.

The endgame came in 1940. In June of that year, New York City bought out the BMT and the IRT and placed them, together with the IND, under the public Board of Transportation—the largest railroad unification in American history to that point, and the definitive conversion of mass transit from private enterprise to public service. The city promptly began demolishing the redundant elevated lines that the subways had superseded. What had begun a century earlier as a private speculation in omnibus routes ended as a municipal department.

The Long Decline

Measured by riders, American mass transit peaked just after World War II. Gasoline and tire rationing had driven wartime ridership to record heights; the New York subway carried over two billion passengers in 1946, and on December 23 of that year set a single-day record of 8,872,249 fares that still stands. Then the decline began, and it did not stop for three decades. The causes were structural. The automobile, cheap and mass-produced since the 1920s, was liberated after the war by rising incomes, federally insured suburban mortgages, and massive public investment in roads—culminating in the Interstate Highway Act of 1956. The new suburbs were built at automobile densities, beyond the reach of any streetcar line, and the new suburban shopping centers meant that trips no longer converged downtown at all. Transit companies, still burdened by franchise obligations and regulated fares, cut service, deferred maintenance, and replaced rail with cheaper buses; each cut drove away more riders in a spiral that economists came to call the transit death spiral. The arc of the whole transit century is visible in a single curve of national ridership—rise, plateau, Depression trough, wartime peak, and collapse (Figure 9.2).

Into this story of structural decline enters one of the most durable legends of American urban history: the General Motors streetcar conspiracy. The facts are these. National City Lines, a bus-operating holding company run by the Fitzgerald brothers, raised capital after 1936 from General Motors, Firestone Tire, Standard Oil of California, Phillips Petroleum, and Mack Trucks, and used it to buy up transit systems—by 1947 it controlled 46 systems in 45 cities, including Los Angeles, Baltimore, St. Louis, and Oakland—and converted many of their streetcar lines to buses supplied by its investors. In 1949 a federal jury convicted GM and its partners of conspiring to monopolize the sale of buses, fuel, and tires to NCL companies; the fines were derisory—$5,000 for GM, one dollar for its treasurer—and the companies were acquitted of the graver charge of conspiring to monopolize the transit industry itself. In 1974, the attorney Bradford Snell told a Senate subcommittee that GM had deliberately destroyed the nation's rail transit to sell cars and buses, and the story—embellished by its retelling in popular culture, most famously in the film Who Framed Roger Rabbit—hardened into folk memory.

Most transit historians reject the conspiracy as an explanation, even while acknowledging the conduct. The NCL properties amounted to perhaps a tenth of American transit systems, and cities NCL never touched abandoned their streetcars on the same schedule; the industry's ruin owed far more to the fixed fare, the Depression, franchise burdens, subsidized highways, and the suburbanizing preferences of millions of households. As the transit writer Guy Span concluded, GM's assault on electric traction was real but "by no means the single reason for the failure of rapid transit." The legend endures, perhaps, because it converts a diffuse structural tragedy—a thousand policy choices and a hundred million private ones—into a story with a villain.

Fogelson's Downtown gives the decline its spatial meaning. Transit had made downtown by pointing every line at it; the automobile unmade downtown by pointing everywhere. Fogelson traces the district's trajectory in three phases—from "the business district" of the 1880s, to "the central business district" of the early twentieth century, to, by mid-century, "just another business district," one node among many in a decentralized metropolis. Downtown's property owners fought decentralization with every tool of what Fogelson calls spatial politics: campaigning for new subways to keep the crowds coming, then for parking garages and elevated highways to accommodate the cars, then for urban redevelopment to clear the "blight" at downtown's edges. Much of it was self-defeating; the highways and parking lots devoured the very density that had made downtown valuable. The deepest irony of the transit century is here: the machines that assembled the American downtown between 1850 and 1920 were succeeded by a machine that, between 1920 and 1960, quietly took it apart.

Parks and Recreation: Nature in the City

How nineteenth- and twentieth-century Americans built landscapes of grass, water, and play into their cities — and fought over who those landscapes were for.

Learning Objectives

  • Trace the lineage of the American urban park from the rural cemetery movement of the 1830s through the large pleasure grounds of the 1850s–1890s to the Progressive-era playground and the mid-century recreation facility.
  • Explain the political campaign for Central Park, the terms of the 1857–58 design competition, and the human cost of assembling the site, including the destruction of Seneca Village.
  • Analyze Frederick Law Olmsted's 1870 address "Public Parks and the Enlargement of Towns" — his diagnosis of urban ills, his categories of exertive, receptive, gregarious, and neighborly recreation, and his claim that parks are democratic institutions.
  • Describe how single parks grew into park systems linked by parkways in Brooklyn, Buffalo, Boston, and Chicago.
  • Evaluate how questions of access — class, race, gender, and age — shaped park use, from Central Park's early rules to segregated pools and the empire of Robert Moses.

On a February evening in 1870, a landscape architect stood before the American Social Science Association in Boston and made an unusual claim: the most urgent public work facing the modern city was not a sewer or a railroad, but a landscape. Frederick Law Olmsted had spent a dozen years building Central Park in New York, and he had watched crowds of fifty and even a hundred thousand people promenade across its meadows. What he saw convinced him that the industrial city was slowly injuring its inhabitants — bodily, mentally, morally — and that a carefully composed piece of scenery could function as a cure. His address, "Public Parks and the Enlargement of Towns," became the founding theoretical statement of American park-making. This chapter follows the idea of urban nature from its improbable birthplace — a cemetery — through the great pleasure grounds of the nineteenth century, into the sandlots and swimming pools of the twentieth, asking throughout the question Olmsted thought he had answered: whose park is it?

Graves Before Greens: The Rural Cemetery Movement

The American park movement began, strangely enough, with the dead. In the early nineteenth century, city burial grounds were overcrowded, unsanitary, and — in an era that blamed epidemics on foul "miasmas" — feared as sources of disease. In 1831 the Massachusetts Horticultural Society, led by the physician-botanist Jacob Bigelow, consecrated Mount Auburn Cemetery on seventy-two acres of rolling, wooded land in Cambridge and Watertown, four miles from Boston. Mount Auburn was the nation's first "rural" cemetery: a landscaped ground of ponds, glades, winding carriage drives, and picturesque monuments, designed as much for the living as for the dead. Its success set off a national imitation — Laurel Hill in Philadelphia (1836), Green-Wood in Brooklyn (1838), and dozens more.

What made the rural cemeteries historically decisive, as David Schuyler emphasizes in his survey of parks in urban America, was not their function but their popularity. City dwellers starved for green space treated them as parks: families picnicked among the tombs, guidebooks were printed, and on fine weekends Green-Wood drew tens of thousands of visitors. Olmsted noticed the phenomenon and found it telling. In his 1870 address he remarked that everywhere in America he had observed "a custom of gregarious out-of-door recreation in some miserably imperfect form, usually covered by a wretched pretext of a wholly different purpose, as perhaps, for instance, visiting a grave-yard." If people would travel miles to stroll in a cemetery, the demand for public landscape was real — and cities ought to meet it honestly, with grounds designed for recreation rather than burial. The rural cemetery was the proto-park: it proved the constituency, trained the first American landscape gardeners, and established the pastoral, contemplative aesthetic the park movement would inherit.

Key Concept: The Pleasure Ground

Historians of park design use the term "pleasure ground" for the first era of American parks (roughly 1850–1900): large, naturalistic landscapes of meadow, wood, and water, usually sited at the urban edge, intended for receptive enjoyment of scenery — strolling, carriage driving, picnicking — rather than organized sport. The pleasure ground assumed that the experience of pastoral scenery itself, working on visitors half-consciously, was the park's public benefit.

The Central Park Experiment

The campaign for a great New York park began in the 1840s, as Manhattan's population surged past 300,000 and its relentless street grid marched northward with almost no provision for open space. In 1844 the poet-editor William Cullen Bryant used his New-York Evening Post to call for a new public park while unbuilt land could still be had. He was soon joined by Andrew Jackson Downing, the country's most influential landscape gardener, whose journal The Horticulturist pressed the case: a park of hundreds of acres, Downing argued, would refine public taste, improve public health, and prove that republican institutions could produce civic art equal to the royal parks of Europe. Downing drowned in a steamboat disaster in 1852, but his English-born partner, the architect Calvert Vaux, carried his ideas into the project. Merchants added a more worldly rationale — a great park would raise property values and advertise the city — and in 1853 the legislature authorized the taking of more than 700 acres in the center of the island (later enlarged to 843).

The land was not empty. Alongside scattered farms, quarries, and bone-boiling works, the park site contained Seneca Village, a settled community on the west side between roughly 82nd and 89th Streets. Founded in 1825, when landowners began selling lots to African American purchasers, Seneca Village had grown by 1855 to about 225 residents — roughly two-thirds Black, the remainder mostly Irish immigrants — with three churches, a school, and gardens. For Black New Yorkers, property there carried special weight: it offered stability remote from downtown hostility and, for men, the $250 in property required to vote under New York's discriminatory franchise law. In 1855–57 the city took the entire site by eminent domain; residents contested the compensation awards but were dispersed, and Seneca Village was erased so completely that its history was recovered only by modern scholars and archaeologists. The episode is a standing reminder, developed in Roy Rosenzweig and Elizabeth Blackmar's history of the park, that the "empty" land of park-making was often someone's home, and that the costs of civic improvement fell first on those with the least political power.

In 1857 the new park board hired Olmsted — then a gentleman-farmer, journalist, and author of famous travel accounts of the slave South — as superintendent of the grounds, and announced a public design competition. Vaux invited Olmsted to collaborate, and in April 1858 their entry, the "Greensward" plan, won first prize among thirty-three submissions. Greensward translated the pastoral ideal into engineering. Sweeping meadows, a naturalistic lake, and the wilder "Ramble" were composed to make the surrounding city disappear from view; the formal Mall, the one architectural set piece, funneled promenaders toward Bethesda Terrace. Most inventively, the plan sank four transverse roads below grade so that crosstown traffic could pass through the park without intruding on it — a separation of circulation systems decades ahead of its time. Building the park was itself an epic of urban labor: thousands of workers moved millions of cubic yards of stone and soil and planted hundreds of thousands of trees, manufacturing "nature" on a blasted, rocky site. The first sections opened to skaters in the winter of 1858–59.

Olmsted's Argument: Parks as Cures for Urban Malaise

Why should a city tax itself millions of dollars for scenery? Olmsted's 1870 Boston address is the fullest answer he ever gave. He began not with parks but with urbanization itself. Against the Jeffersonian instinct that cities were aberrations, Olmsted insisted that the "townward drift" of population was permanent, worldwide, and on balance good; the task, he told his social-scientist audience, was "to prepare for a continued rising of the townward flood" — not to lament the city but to plan it.

Yet the city as built exacted a price. Part of the damage was physical: drawing on the sanitary science of his day, Olmsted observed that the air of dense districts was depleted and fouled, carrying "into the lungs highly corrupt and irritating matters" that sapped vitality. But his most original diagnosis was psychological. Urban life, he argued, forced people into ceaseless, guarded calculation about strangers:

Whenever we walk through the denser part of a town, to merely avoid collision with those we meet and pass upon the sidewalks, we have constantly to watch, to foresee, and to guard against their movements. This involves a consideration of their intentions, a calculation of their strength and weakness, which is not so much for their benefit as our own. Our minds are thus brought into close dealings with other minds without any friendly flowing toward them, but rather a drawing from them.

— Frederick Law Olmsted, Public Parks and the Enlargement of Towns (1870)

Commerce compounded the strain, breeding "a tendency to regard others in a hard if not always hardening way." People raised "in the streets" often displayed "a peculiarly hard sort of selfishness": they had brushed against thousands of their fellow beings and "yet have had no experience of anything in common with them." The nervous exhaustion and social coldness of city life were, in Olmsted's account, environmental diseases — and therefore treatable by environmental means. "Air is disinfected by sunlight and foliage," he explained; if the city could offer frequent, convenient escape into purified air and open space, free of the "vigilance, wariness, and activity toward other men" that streets demanded, "our problem would be solved."

The address then built a taxonomy of recreation that became the working vocabulary of the profession. All recreation, Olmsted proposed, is either exertive — active pursuits such as baseball, cricket, or chess — or receptive, in which "we receive pleasure without conscious exertion," as with music, fine art, or scenery. Receptive recreation subdivides again: it is gregarious when the pleasure lies in the sheer presence of a large congregation of people, and neighborly when it flourishes in small, intimate groups — the family picnic, "the easy conversation of the more sedate" mingled with "the prattle of the children." Different recreations demand different spaces. Exertive play could be served by "numerous small grounds so distributed through a large town that some one of them could be easily reached by a short walk from every house" — a startling anticipation of the playground movement. But gregarious recreation on a metropolitan scale required the large park, and there, Olmsted testified, the park revealed its democratic character. Describing the promenades of Central Park and Brooklyn's new park, he offered the most famous passage of the address:

You may thus often see vast numbers of persons brought closely together, poor and rich, young and old, Jew and Gentile. I have seen a hundred thousand thus congregated, and I assure you that though there have been not a few that seemed a little dazed, as if they did not quite understand it, and were, perhaps, a little ashamed of it, I have looked studiously but vainly among them for a single face completely unsympathetic with the prevailing expression of good nature and light-heartedness.

— Frederick Law Olmsted, Public Parks and the Enlargement of Towns (1870)

Here was the park as social infrastructure: the one place in the divided industrial city where classes met "with a common purpose, not at all intellectual, competitive with none... each individual adding by his mere presence to the pleasure of all others." Olmsted claimed measurable moral effects. Citing the park's near-total freedom from vandalism, he argued that it exercised "a distinctly harmonizing and refining influence upon the most unfortunate and most lawless classes of the city, — an influence favorable to courtesy, self-control, and temperance." Whether or not one accepts the causal claim, the ambition is unmistakable: the park was to do for the metropolis what the church and village green had done for the town.

Finally, the address laid down the aesthetic law of the pleasure ground. The park was not to compete with the city's architecture but to supply what buildings never could:

The park should, as far as possible, complement the town. Openness is the one thing you cannot get in buildings... It should be the beauty of the fields, the meadow, the prairie, of the green pastures, and the still waters. What we want to gain is tranquillity and rest to the mind.

— Frederick Law Olmsted, Public Parks and the Enlargement of Towns (1870)

Rugged, "picturesque" scenery was to be avoided — "mountains suggest effort" — as were gardenesque flower displays and iron fences, which treated "the public as prisoners or wild beasts." The great object of park art, he said, "is to influence the mind of men through their imagination": the landscape was to work quietly, half-consciously, on its visitors. That idea — that designed scenery acts unconsciously on the mind — is Olmsted's deepest theoretical legacy, and it explains why he fought throughout his career to protect broad meadows from encroachment by museums, monuments, and ball fields.

Key Concept: Receptive vs. Exertive Recreation

Olmsted's distinction between recreation that stimulates exertion (sport, games) and recreation received "without conscious exertion" (the contemplation of scenery) was more than a classification: it was a design program and a political position. The large pastoral park privileged the receptive mode; later reformers who put gymnastics, playgrounds, and pools at the center of park policy were, in effect, reversing Olmsted's priority — a shift from nature as medicine to play as medicine.

From Park to Park System

Central Park's success made it the most imitated public work of the century, and Olmsted and Vaux's later commissions pushed the idea beyond the single park toward the planned metropolitan system. Prospect Park in Brooklyn (designed 1865–66) is generally judged their masterpiece — its Long Meadow, Ravine, and lake a more unified pastoral composition than Central Park's — but its real innovation lay at its edges. To connect the park to distant neighborhoods and the sea, the partners proposed broad, tree-lined pleasure drives and coined a word for them: parkways. Eastern Parkway and Ocean Parkway extended the park's influence into the fabric of the growing city, exactly as the 1870 address urged when it imagined local grounds "connected and supplemented by a series of trunk-roads or boulevards."

In Buffalo, beginning in 1868, Olmsted and Vaux designed a system from the start: three differentiated grounds — a large pastoral park, a formal parade ground, and a waterfront park — distributed across the city and stitched together by two-hundred-foot-wide parkways. Olmsted later showcased Buffalo as the best-planned city in America. In Boston, from 1878 into the 1890s, he designed the chain that came to be called the Emerald Necklace, running from the Common and Public Garden through the Back Bay Fens, the Riverway, and Jamaica Pond to the Arnold Arboretum and the great country park at Franklin Park. The Fens marked something new: nominally a park, it was equally sanitary engineering — a designed tidal marsh that cured the flooding and sewage stench of a polluted creek. Landscape architecture, Olmsted was demonstrating, was infrastructure.

Chicago tells the story's next turn. In 1869 the Illinois legislature created three park commissions for the city, and Olmsted and Vaux prepared the 1871 plan for the South Park Commission's 1,055 acres — the future Washington Park and Jackson Park, joined by the mile-long Midway Plaisance. Jackson Park's lakefront site lay largely unrealized until Chicago won the World's Columbian Exposition, which Olmsted, with Daniel Burnham, laid out there for 1893; after the fair the ground reverted to parkland. Then, in the early 1900s, the South Park Commission did something Olmsted had only sketched: it built a network of small neighborhood parks in the dense immigrant districts of the South Side, each with a "fieldhouse" containing gymnasiums, baths, clubrooms, and branch libraries, set among playgrounds and ball fields. These reform parks carried park policy decisively from scenery toward service, and they signal the transition to the movement examined next.

The Playground Turn

By the 1890s a new generation of reformers had concluded that the great pleasure grounds, whatever their beauty, were failing the people who needed them most. Working-class families in tenement districts could not easily reach parks sited at the urban edge, and park rules — keep off the grass, no organized games without permits — fit poorly with the needs of children. The playground movement began humbly: in 1885, inspired by reports of public sand piles in Berlin, Boston charity workers placed a heap of sand in the yard of the Parmenter Street Chapel in the immigrant North End. The "sand gardens" multiplied, acquired matrons and organized programs, and seeded a national crusade. In Chicago, Hull House opened a model playground in 1894; in New York, the journalist Jacob Riis and the reformer Charles Stover campaigned for small parks in the tenement wards, and in 1903 Seward Park on the Lower East Side opened as the first permanent municipally built playground in the country.

Progressive-era playground advocates shared Olmsted's environmentalism — the belief that surroundings shape character — but inverted his method: where the pleasure ground worked through receptive contemplation, the playground worked through supervised, strenuous play. Reformers such as Joseph Lee, the Boston philanthropist known as the father of the American playground movement, argued that play was not idleness but the central instrument of child development and civic education: organized games taught teamwork, self-government, and loyalty, and offered immigrant children an alternative to the street, the gang, and the juvenile court. Lee's Play in Education (1915) gave the movement its philosophy; the Playground Association of America, founded in 1906 with the physical educator Luther Gulick as president and Jane Addams as a vice president — Lee later led it for decades — gave it a professional apparatus of surveys, standards, and trained recreation workers. Municipal playgrounds, athletic fields, baths, and swimming pools proliferated through the 1900s and 1910s — the number of American cities with supervised playgrounds grew more than twentyfold between 1900 and 1917 (see Figure 10.2) — and by the 1930s the transformation was complete: the typical new "park" was a recreation facility of asphalt, apparatus, ball fields, and pool, and Olmsted's meadows were increasingly carved up for exactly the active uses he had resisted.

Whose Park? Access and Exclusion

Olmsted advertised the park as the one ground where "poor and rich, young and old, Jew and Gentile" mingled freely, and by European standards the American park was strikingly open. But the democratic claim always had limits. In its early decades Central Park was most easily enjoyed by those with leisure and carriage fare; its rules against ball-playing and walking on the grass bore hardest on working-class visitors, and only gradually, under popular pressure, did concerts move to Sundays — the workers' one free day — and restrictions relax. The displacement of Seneca Village had already shown that the park's "public" was constructed by force as well as by invitation.

Race drew the sharpest line. In the Jim Crow South, municipal parks and playgrounds were segregated by law or custom — many cities provided Black residents no parks at all, or a single inferior ground — and segregation extended to the newest and most intimate park spaces: swimming pools and beaches. In the urban North, as the historian Jeff Wiltse has shown, early municipal pools were divided by sex but often used by Black and white bathers alike; when cities merged men's and women's swimming in the 1920s, whites — policing the prospect of interracial, mixed-sex intimacy — imposed racial exclusion instead, through official policy in some cities and through harassment and violence in others. Recreational space could be a flashpoint: Chicago's 1919 race riot began at a Lake Michigan beach, where a Black teenager, Eugene Williams, drifted across an invisible color line and was stoned and drowned, and white crowds attacked Black swimmers at pools such as St. Louis's Fairground Park as late as 1949. After the Second World War, when courts ordered public recreation desegregated, many southern cities drained or closed their pools — Montgomery, Alabama, shut its entire parks system in 1959 rather than integrate — while private "swim clubs" absorbed white demand. The park, conceived as the great mixing ground of urban democracy, had become one of the places where Americans fought hardest to avoid mixing.

The parks story of this course ends with Robert Moses, who became New York City's first citywide parks commissioner in 1934 and, through a shifting collection of state and city offices held over four decades, built public recreation on a scale no one had imagined: hundreds of new playgrounds within a few years, eleven enormous WPA-built swimming pools opened in the single summer of 1936, Jones Beach, and hundreds of miles of landscaped parkways that fused the Olmstedian pleasure drive with the automobile age. Moses represents both the fulfillment and the inversion of the tradition this chapter has traced. Fulfillment, because he delivered mass recreation to millions with New Deal labor and unmatched administrative skill; inversion, because his parks were engineered facilities rather than pastoral retreats, his parkways served drivers rather than strollers, and access again tracked power — his biographer Robert Caro and later scholars have documented how few of his early playgrounds went to Harlem, and how siting, transit, and policing choices limited who could reach his grandest facilities. When postwar critics like Jane Jacobs attacked Moses's park-flanked expressways, they were reopening Olmsted's question for a new century: what does it mean to build nature into the city, and for whom?

Inside the Dumbbell

Climb the stoop of a tenement on Orchard Street around 1900 and step into the entrance hall: a corridor less than three feet wide, running sixty feet back into the building, and — even at noon — almost totally dark. Feel your way up the stairs. On each floor above the ground-floor shops are fourteen rooms, divided into four apartments; of those fourteen rooms, only four have a window on the street or the ten-foot back yard. The rest open onto the "air shaft," a slot barely twenty-eight inches wide between this building and the identical one next door, enclosed on all four sides and rising sixty or seventy feet to a small square of sky. The shaft delivers not air but odors, noise, and — when fire breaks out — flame, for it acts as a flue. In the front apartment a family of six pays twelve to eighteen dollars a month for four rooms, two of which are closet-sized bedrooms where, in the words of the state commission that studied them, sleep is often impossible; on hot nights the family drags mattresses to the parlor floor, the roof, or the fire escape. Two water-closets in the public hall serve four families. This is the "double-decker" or dumbbell tenement — named for its pinched-waist floor plan — and in 1900 it was, block after block, the standard home of most New Yorkers.

This chapter asks how American urban institutions confronted that building and everything it stood for. The story unfolds in three acts. First, Progressive reformers led by Lawrence Veiller attacked the tenement with restrictive legislation — the landmark New York Tenement House Act of 1901 — while pointedly rejecting the European alternative of city-built housing. Second, after decades of debate that historian Margaret Garb has reconstructed, the federal government finally entered the housing business in the 1930s, creating the public housing program whose rise and unraveling D. Bradford Hunt has traced. Third, running alongside both, an escape route: the early suburbs described by Ann Durkin Keating, which drained the city of those residents — disproportionately white and middle-class — whom lenders, developers, and federal underwriters were willing to help leave. Housing reform, public housing, and suburbanization were not separate stories; they were three answers, unequally available, to the same question the dumbbell posed.

The Tenement Problem

New York had regulated tenements before. A "tenement house" was legally any building housing three or more families living independently; the first Tenement House Act, in 1867, required fire escapes and at least one privy for every twenty occupants. In 1879, after a competition sponsored by the trade journal Sanitary Engineer, a new law required that every sleeping room have a window opening to the outer air. The competition's winning design, by architect James E. Ware, satisfied that rule on the standard 25-by-100-foot lot by indenting the side walls to create the narrow air shaft — and the dumbbell was born. Tellingly, the award was condemned almost immediately: the New York Times attacked the prize plans in March 1879, and reformers would spend the next two decades documenting what the commission later called a type "which New York has the unenviable distinction of having invented." Buildings erected under the 1879 statute became known as Old Law tenements, and roughly a generation of them — tens of thousands of buildings — filled Manhattan below Houston Street and far beyond.

Jacob Riis's How the Other Half Lives (1890) had made tenement conditions a public scandal; the task of turning scandal into law fell to Lawrence Veiller (1872–1959), a young settlement-trained reformer who became secretary of the Charity Organization Society's Tenement House Committee in 1898. Veiller's genius was for evidence and drafting. His Tenement House Exhibition of 1899–1900 — maps of disease and poverty, photographs, and cardboard models showing entire blocks of dumbbells — drew thousands of visitors, including Governor Theodore Roosevelt, who in 1900 appointed a State Tenement House Commission chaired by the philanthropist-lawyer Robert W. DeForest, with Veiller as secretary. The commission's two-volume report, published in 1903 as The Tenement House Problem, remains the single richest portrait of American slum housing ever assembled. It counted 82,652 tenement houses in Greater New York, home to 2,372,079 people — more than two-thirds of the city's population. In the Tenth Ward, the heart of the Jewish Lower East Side, 76,073 tenement dwellers were packed onto 109 acres — roughly seven hundred people per acre, a density the report judged among the highest in the world. Its description of the standard building deserves quoting at length:

Generally, along each side of the building is what is termed an "air shaft," being an indentation of the wall to a depth of about 28 inches, and extending in length for a space of from 50 to 60 feet. This shaft is entirely enclosed on four sides, and is, of course, the full height of the building, often from 60 to 72 feet high. The ostensible purpose of the shaft is to provide light and air to the five rooms on each side of the house which get no direct light and air from the street or yard; but as the shafts are narrow and high, being enclosed on all four sides, and without any intake of air at the bottom, these rooms obtain, instead of fresh air and sunshine, foul air and semi-darkness. … [E]ach family, besides having the foul air from its own rooms to breathe, is compelled to breathe the emanations from the rooms of some eleven other families; nor is this all, these shafts act as conveyors of noise, odors, and disease, and when fire breaks out serve as inflammable flues, often rendering it impossible to save the buildings from destruction.

— Robert W. DeForest and Lawrence Veiller, eds., The Tenement House Problem (1903)

The report's indictment went beyond discomfort. Dark rooms, it argued, bred tuberculosis — the "tubercle bacillus," it noted, "is readily destroyed by sunlight, or even diffused daylight" — and it found hardly a tenement house in the city without at least one case of the disease in the previous five years. The commission summarized the evils to be remedied as "insufficiency of light and air due to narrow courts or air shafts, undue height, and to the occupation by the building or by the adjacent buildings of too great proportion of the lot area; danger from fire; lack of separate water-closet and washing facilities; overcrowding; foul cellars and courts." Note what that list is: a list of building defects, correctable by law. That framing was Veiller's, and it determined the American approach to housing for the next thirty years.

Key Concept: The Dumbbell Tenement

The dumbbell was not the product of greed alone but of regulation interacting with the lot. The 1879 law demanded a window in every bedroom; the 25-by-100-foot lot made a full courtyard unprofitable; the air shaft was the cheapest legal compromise. The lesson reformers drew — that badly written rules can institutionalize a bad building for a generation — explains the New Law's obsessive specificity about courts, yards, and lot coverage.

Legislating Light and Air: The New Law of 1901

The commission chose not merely to recommend but to legislate: it drafted the Tenement House Act that New York State enacted in April 1901, published for builders in editions such as William J. Fryer's The Tenement House Law of the City of New York (1901). The statute's language shows how completely reformers now distrusted builders' discretion. Under the heading "Light and Ventilation," Section 51 commanded that "no tenement house hereafter erected shall occupy more than ninety per centum of a corner lot, or more than seventy per centum of any other lot." Height could not exceed the width of the widest adjoining street by more than one-third. The air shaft was abolished outright: new buildings required genuine courts of specified minimum widths that grew with the building's height, and rear yards of at least ten to twelve feet. Any tenement over five stories or sixty-seven feet had to be fireproof. Section 95 required "a separate water-closet in a separate compartment within each apartment," each with a window to the open air. Even privacy was legislated: in every apartment of three or more rooms, access to each bedroom and to the toilet "shall be had without passing through any bedroom."

Crucially, the New Law reached backward as well as forward. Existing Old Law buildings had to cut windows into interior rooms, light their public halls, and — under Section 100 — remove every "school sink" and privy vault by January 1903, replacing them with individual, sewer-connected water-closets, at least one for every two families. And the law solved the enforcement problem that had crippled every earlier statute by creating a dedicated municipal agency, the Tenement House Department (1902), with power to approve plans before construction and to inspect the city's entire tenement stock. DeForest became its first commissioner, Veiller his deputy. The results were visible within a few years. Looking back in the report's introduction, DeForest and Veiller allowed themselves a rare note of triumph: "The discredited and horrible 'dumb-bell' tenement, the prevailing type of house built in New York from 1879 until 1901, is now a thing of the past. At one stroke it was wiped out of existence as a type of future multiple dwelling." In its place rose the "New Law tenement," with large courts, light halls, and a private toilet in every apartment — the six-story walk-up that still lines the streets of upper Manhattan, Brooklyn, and the Bronx.

The New Law's success made it a national template. Veiller founded the National Housing Association in 1910 and published model tenement-house codes that dozens of cities and states adapted. A related regulatory innovation arrived in 1916, when New York adopted the nation's first comprehensive zoning resolution, dividing the whole city into use, height, and area districts. Zoning grew from different anxieties — Fifth Avenue merchants alarmed by encroaching garment lofts, and the massive shadow of the 1915 Equitable Building — but it shared the New Law's underlying logic: the city would not build, but it would prescribe, in ever-finer detail, what private builders could do with light, air, and land. Regulation, not provision, was becoming the signature American housing institution.

Why No Municipal Housing? Garb and the Road Not Taken

Here is the puzzle at the center of Margaret Garb's "Municipal Housing in America." In the same decades that New York perfected restrictive legislation, European cities were building homes. London's county council erected model dwellings such as the Boundary Street estate in the 1890s; Glasgow, Liverpool, and Manchester ran municipal tenements; after World War I, Britain subsidized council housing on a mass scale and socialist Vienna built tens of thousands of workers' flats. American reformers knew all this intimately — The Tenement House Problem itself devotes chapters and photographs to London's and Manchester's municipal model tenements. Yet no major American city followed. Why?

Garb's answer begins with the reformers themselves. American housing reform was led not by labor parties, as in much of Europe, but by charity organizations, businessmen, and lawyers who saw decent housing as a matter of market correction and moral uplift, not social right. Three strategies competed. Model tenements — limited-dividend buildings financed by "philanthropy and five percent" investors such as Alfred T. White in Brooklyn — proved that sanitary housing could pay modest returns, but never scaled beyond a few thousand units. Municipal housing, championed by a minority of reformers and, later, by "housers" such as Edith Elmer Wood and Catherine Bauer, promised quantity but collided with American convictions about property, taxes, and the corruptibility of city governments — no small fear in the age of Tammany. Restrictive legislation, Veiller's method, won because it demanded no public money, threatened no private builder's market, and fit the era's faith that law could perfect the market rather than replace it. The 1900 commission made the case against city-built housing in words worth reading closely, because they governed American policy for a generation:

The Commission is not prepared to recommend such an enlargement of municipal functions. … At most such public buildings would better the living conditions of a favored few, who had sufficient influence to secure apartments in them, and even these would better their living conditions at the sacrifice of self-dependence. … [P]rivate enterprise will not compete with municipal bounty, and when cities begin to build tenements other tenement building will cease. No large city can provide homes for all its working people. … If cities, however, are to become landlords at all, where should the wage line be drawn between those for whom they should and those for whom they should not provide? … If tenanted with a view to votes, they might be so located and utilized by the political party in power as to perpetuate its control.

— Robert W. DeForest and Lawrence Veiller, eds., The Tenement House Problem (1903)

Every anxiety of American political culture is packed into that paragraph: fear of dependency, fear of crowding out private enterprise, fear of an unlimited entitlement, fear of machine politics. The commission even rejected the milder tool of tax exemptions for model tenements as too great a departure from equal taxation. Garb emphasizes that the argument was never truly settled — trade unionists, socialists, and women reformers kept municipal housing alive as a demand through the 1910s and 1920s, and New York State experimented with limited-dividend incentives in the 1920s — but until the Great Depression shattered confidence in the private market, regulation remained America's answer. When government housing finally came, it arrived bearing the commission's fingerprints: means-tested, minimal, and designed above all not to compete with private builders.

The Public Housing Experiment: Hunt

D. Bradford Hunt's "Public Housing in Urban America" picks up the story where Garb's leaves off, and his framing is important: public housing was not a single policy but a layered experiment whose early constraints predicted its later crises. The first breach in the wall came during World War I, when munitions and shipyard workers overwhelmed industrial towns and the federal government — through the U.S. Housing Corporation and the Emergency Fleet Corporation — built or financed housing for tens of thousands of war workers, including well-designed communities such as Yorkship Village, New Jersey. The precedent was deliberately buried: Congress ordered the projects sold off after the Armistice. But reformers had seen the state build good housing, and the housers of the interwar years — above all Catherine Bauer, whose Modern Housing (1934) held up European social housing as the model — did not forget it.

The Depression reopened the door. The Public Works Administration's Housing Division (1933) built some fifty projects — about 21,600 units — directly with federal funds, including Atlanta's Techwood Homes, dedicated by President Roosevelt in 1935 as the nation's first federal public housing project, and Harlem River Houses in New York, built for Black tenants. New York City created its own housing authority in 1934, and its modest First Houses on the Lower East Side opened in December 1935 — fittingly, a rehabilitation carved out of old tenement blocks. Then came the decisive statute: the Wagner-Steagall Housing Act of September 1937, which created the United States Housing Authority (USHA) to lend up to ninety percent of project costs to local housing authorities — a new species of institution, appointed boards chartered under state law — and to subsidize rents down to levels the poor could pay.

Hunt insists that the act's compromises mattered as much as its ambitions. To pacify the real estate lobby and honor the old fear of competing with private enterprise, the program was aimed exclusively at the poor, with strict income ceilings that evicted upwardly mobile tenants. An "equivalent elimination" clause required that for every public unit built, a slum unit be demolished or repaired — welding public housing to slum clearance rather than to expanding the housing supply. Construction cost caps (in the 1937 act, $4,000 per unit and $1,000 per room, slightly higher in the largest cities) enforced a deliberate austerity: no closets doors here, no ornament, nothing that might make a project nicer than the private market. And localism gave city politics control of the two most fateful decisions — who would live in projects, and where they would stand. In the 1930s and 1940s the results still looked like success: solid low-rise projects, carefully selected working-poor tenants (the "submerged middle class"), long waiting lists.

The postwar decades turned constraints into catastrophe, a process Hunt has examined most closely in Chicago in his book Blueprint for Disaster. The Housing Act of 1949 authorized 810,000 new units and vast urban redevelopment, but site selection now collided with race. White neighborhoods and suburban councils vetoed projects; Black neighborhoods, and cleared Black slums, got them — so public housing re-concentrated the very segregation and poverty it was meant to relieve. In Chicago, the city council systematically rejected the housing authority's vacant-land sites in white areas, confining projects to the ghetto. Cost caps plus expensive inner-city land pushed design upward: the elevator high-rise, cheap per unit and disastrous for family life, became the default, culminating in monoliths like Chicago's Robert Taylor Homes and St. Louis's Pruitt-Igoe — thirty-three eleven-story slabs, about 2,870 units, opened in 1954–1956. Financing completed the trap: federal money paid for construction, but maintenance had to come from tenants' rents, so as cities deindustrialized and projects filled with the poorest households, revenue collapsed just as buildings aged. Pruitt-Igoe, half-empty and unmaintainable, was dynamited beginning in 1972 — an image endlessly replayed as proof that public housing "failed." As the 2011 documentary The Pruitt-Igoe Myth argues, the demolition footage obscures the real causes: a shrinking, segregated St. Louis, a funding formula designed to starve, and rules written in 1937 to protect the private market. The experiment was not disproven; it was never permitted to succeed on the terms European social housing enjoyed.

Key Concept: Design Follows Finance

Public housing's towers are often blamed on architects' modernist ideology, and Le Corbusier's "towers in the park" did supply the imagery. But Hunt shows the deeper logic was institutional: statutory cost caps, expensive slum-clearance sites, and per-unit accounting made the elevator high-rise the rational choice for local authorities. When you see a project tower, read it as a budget, a site-selection fight, and a racial boundary — not merely a style.

Escaping the City: Early Suburbs

While reformers regulated the tenement and housers fought for projects, a third institution was quietly deciding the metropolitan future: the suburb. Ann Durkin Keating's survey of suburbanization before 1945 reminds us that the suburb long predates the automobile. Ferry commuters made Brooklyn Heights a genteel refuge from Manhattan by the 1820s; railroads spawned elite enclaves like Llewellyn Park, New Jersey (1857) and Olmsted's Riverside outside Chicago (1869). The electric streetcar after 1888 democratized the exit, opening "streetcar suburbs" of modest houses to clerks and skilled workers along the new radial lines — the process Sam Bass Warner traced in Boston. Suburbs were never only residential: industrial satellites and immigrant workers' subdivisions grew alongside the picturesque commuter towns, and Keating stresses this diversity against the stereotype of the leafy bedroom community.

The institutional hinge was annexation. Through most of the nineteenth century, cities simply absorbed their fringes — Chicago's mammoth 1889 annexation added well over a hundred square miles — because outlying communities wanted city water, sewers, and fire protection. But when Brookline, Massachusetts voted in 1873 to refuse consolidation with Boston, it signaled a new era: as suburbs learned to buy services through special districts or their own incorporated governments, they chose political independence, and annexation largely halted in the Northeast and Midwest by the 1920s. The metropolis fractured into a central city ringed by self-governing suburbs that could tax, zone, and exclude on their own terms. Exclusion had private instruments too — deed restrictions and racially restrictive covenants that barred sale to Black families and often to Jews and Asians — and after 1934 a federal one: the Federal Housing Administration, created by the National Housing Act, insured long-term amortized mortgages but rated neighborhoods for risk in the manner of the Home Owners' Loan Corporation's color-coded maps, which marked minority and mixed areas in red. FHA's underwriting manuals favored new, homogeneous, covenant-protected subdivisions — "redlining" older and Black neighborhoods out of the mortgage market. The same federal government that capped public housing's budgets was thus subsidizing white suburban homeownership, sorting the metropolis by race and class. The high-rise project and the covenanted subdivision were twin products of one policy regime.

Talking to Tenants: For You and the Institutional Voice

Laws and loan programs addressed buildings and markets; one last document lets us hear a housing institution addressing a person. Around 1914 the Tenement House Department, together with the Charity Organization Society's Tenement House Committee, issued a little illustrated guide for tenement dwellers titled For You; it went through repeated printings, and the third edition of 1917 — thirty-two pages, distributed by the tens of thousands — carried the homely motto on its cover: "It is hard to get money. It is harder to spend it right. Health is wealth." Where the New Law spoke to builders in the imperative of statute, For You spoke to tenants in the second person, "telling in the simplest possible way the things that tenants ought to know": open your windows for fresh air, keep the rooms and halls clean, guard the baby's milk, do not take in more boarders than the rooms can bear, and report broken plumbing or a darkened hall to the department — whose inspectors, the pamphlet made clear, worked for the tenant as much as against the landlord. The National Housing Association's journal Housing Betterment recorded both the pamphlet's reach and the philosophy behind it:

The New York Department has been one of the first to realize that beyond a certain point, further progress in improving living conditions is painfully slow, unless means can be found to prevail upon tenants to do their share. The Tenement House Department in conjunction with the Tenement House Committee of the Charity Organization Society prepared a pamphlet called "For You," telling in the simplest possible way the things that tenants ought to know. Nearly one hundred thousand copies have already been given out through schools, settlements and various other social agencies. The experiment has proved a marked success and the Commissioner ends his latest report with the following comment: "The Department believes that if it is to accomplish its purpose — the development of safe and sanitary living conditions in New York City — it must devote some portion of its time and funds to further educational work along the same line."

— National Housing Association, Housing Betterment (1914–1918)

Read one way, For You is Progressive paternalism in pocket form — city officials and charity workers instructing immigrant mothers in American standards of ventilation and thrift, shifting some responsibility for slum conditions onto the people who endured them. Read another way, it marks something genuinely new in urban institutional history: a municipal department that conceived of tenants as its constituents, told them their legal rights along with their duties, and printed the message in language (and in other cities that copied it, in translations) meant to reach them where they lived. The distance from the dark hallway of 1900 to a city agency mailing health advice "for you" measures how far the institutional relationship between the city and its poorest residents had traveled in one generation — and how far it still had to go, as the public housing decades would prove.

Discussion Questions

  1. The dumbbell tenement was itself the product of an 1879 reform law. What does its history suggest about how regulations shape the built environment, and can you identify a modern parallel where well-intentioned rules produced a perverse standard building type?
  2. The 1900 commission argued that municipal housing would reward "a favored few," sap "self-dependence," and be captured by machine politics. Which of these objections were reasonable predictions in 1900, and which were ideology? How did the public housing program after 1937 confirm or refute them?
  3. Garb frames America's rejection of municipal housing as a choice, not an inevitability. What coalition, at what moment between 1900 and 1937, might plausibly have pushed the United States toward the European model — and what blocked it?
  4. Hunt argues that public housing's failures were built into its founding statute — income limits, cost caps, equivalent elimination, local control — rather than caused by tenants or towers. Test this claim against the case of Pruitt-Igoe: how much of its collapse can be traced to 1937?
  5. Consider FHA mortgage insurance and USHA project subsidies as two halves of one federal housing policy. How did they jointly sort the metropolis by race and class, and how does Keating's account of pre-1945 suburban political independence deepen the story?
  6. Read the For You pamphlet as an institutional voice. What does its blend of health advice, moral instruction, and rights information reveal about how Progressive institutions understood tenement dwellers — as victims, threats, pupils, or citizens?

Course Companion: Assignments and Exercises

The complete apparatus of the seminar — weekly reflections, the Pick-a-City exercise, the midterm debate, and the final paper — adapted from the Spring 2025 course materials.

How the Seminar Works

The course meets once a week for a required two-hour discussion seminar, with an optional one-hour session for guest speakers, student presentations, and research and writing workshops. There is no final exam. Total written output across the semester — weekly responses, exercises, and the final paper — comes to roughly 20–25 pages. Grading in the original course: weekly responses 35% (seven at 5% each), Pick-a-City exercise 5%, midterm in-class exercise 10%, final paper proposal 5%, final paper 25%, and class participation 20%.

Weekly Reflections

Each reflection is two to three tight paragraphs responding to the week’s prompt, due before class. Reflections are required for seven of the nine prompted weeks (the student’s choice); extra submissions earn modest additional credit. The prompts, adapted from the course:

Week 1 — Urbanization and Industrialization
Louis Wirth describes urbanism and how people and societies adapt to it. Do the week’s readings support, qualify, refute, deepen, or complicate his analysis? How do industrialization and urbanization relate — to what extent, and in which direction does the causation run?
Week 4 — Urban Migration and Diversity
Later weeks examine mobility within cities; this week’s readings show mobility at continental scale. How did mass migrations shape the modern city and the experience of its residents? Wirth stressed heterogeneity — cities are diverse, not merely large. Drawing on the primary sources, reflect on both the benefits and the challenges of urban diversity.
Week 5 — Urban Politics
Using examples from insiders like George Washington Plunkitt, Richard Croker, and Big Tim Sullivan, identify which aspects of machine politics would genuinely appeal to urban voters — and what concerns this system of governance raises.
Week 6 — Reformers
The machine met real needs, especially for immigrant and working-class residents. How did this week’s social reformers intend to meet those same needs, and what systems and cultural practices do their visions require? Focus on one or two examples.
Week 8 — Work and Play
Pick one specific form of nightlife or entertainment from the readings and discuss how it deepens your understanding of earlier themes — industrialization, technology, diversity, capitalism.
Week 9 — Order and Disorder
Unlike parks or schools, this week’s services are different in kind: the people being policed or otherwise controlled might not regard these services as a benefit. Is this a new dynamic between residents and city government? Does it change your view of government’s growing scope?
Week 10 — Public Services and Safety Nets
Are water, sewers, schools, juvenile courts, and public health an extension of “order and disorder,” or a different vision of the government–resident relationship? Which framing fits better, and why?
Week 11 — Urban Mobility
Compare transit to services from prior weeks — similarities and differences in scale, delivery, management, development effects, and who is served. How do physical mobility and social mobility connect in cities?
Week 12 — Parks for the People
Compare parks and open-space amenities to earlier topics, especially public health. Bonus (in the original course): visit Boston’s Public Garden and reflect on how its design and management embody the readings’ ideals.

The Pick-a-City Exercise

Early in the term, each student chooses a leading nineteenth-century American city and writes a brief (2–3 page) summary of its growth and development across the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. The brief should read as a compelling narrative — “focus on the grand arc and the pivotal moments, not every detail” — while answering four questions: Why is the city located where it is? When was it settled, how did it grow, and why? Who lives there and what do they do? What challenges did its growth involve, and how did it respond? Close with a paragraph on something in the city’s history you found especially interesting, and why. A superb aid for choosing: the “Rank of the Most Populous Cities at Each Census, 1790–1890” chart from the 1898 Statistical Atlas of the United States, available from the Library of Congress.

Midterm Exercise: Machines vs. Reformers

In place of an exam, the course stages a debate on the theme “Who Does More to Help Our Cities?” Students are assigned at the start of class as Boss Politicians, Progressive Reformers, or Moderators (who pose pointed questions and offer commentary). The two camps opposed each other bitterly — and, as the exercise slyly notes, “may have more in common than they might be aware of.” The debate proceeds in three rounds:

Debate Rounds

  1. Description and advocacy. What real problems did urban residents face from 1850 to 1930? How should they be solved? What counts as “good government” from your side’s point of view?
  2. Critique. What is wrong with the opposing side’s solutions and approach?
  3. Synthesis. Where do the camps overlap? Which urban groups are excluded by both? What problems does neither side address?

Preparation rules in the original course: use textual evidence from the readings; bring notes and readings to class; up to two pages of prepared notes may be submitted beforehand and count toward the grade along with in-class contributions.

The Final Paper

The term paper (roughly 8–10 pages, footnotes and full bibliography, any citation format) mixes class material with outside research in both primary and secondary sources, and proceeds in three stages: a list of two or three candidate topics (week 7), a one-page proposal with two research questions and at least two primary and two secondary sources identified (week 9), and the finished paper (final week), presented to the class in the closing sessions. The instructor’s advice: papers may capture a single moment or trace a feature of urban life as it emerged or declined, and “the more specific and narrow your topic, the more you’ll be able to really dig in and say something.” Sample topics from the course:

Suggested Paper Topics

  • The end of horse-based transportation in American cities — when, how, and why
  • A twentieth-century social history of the boarding house
  • The Boston police strike of 1919
  • Housing segregation patterns in Baltimore and Washington, DC before and after World War II
  • A comparison of the histories of two urban police departments
  • The early history of the YMCA
