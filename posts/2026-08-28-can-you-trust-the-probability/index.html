Can you trust the probability?

A fraud model scores a $10,000 transaction at 0.95 and you recommend blocking it. Then someone asks: what if only 60% of transactions scored 0.95 turn out to be fraud? That gap is the whole topic. The model ranked the transaction as very risky, but the number 0.95 was not a probability you could take literally.

Ranking risk vs measuring risk

Most people treat .predict_proba() as a true probability. It usually is not one. XGBoost and deep nets are trained and tuned to separate classes, and their scores often come out miscalibrated: 0.90 means "the model is confident", not "90% of cases like this are positive". A model can be excellent at ranking (high AUC) while its scores are badly off as frequencies. Ranking answers "which transactions are riskier than which". Measuring answers "how often is a 0.8 actually fraud". Automated decisions need the second one.

GOTCHA · WHY IT SKEWS
Fraud training data is heavily downsampled on the negative class in most pipelines. Undersampling shifts the base rate the model sees, so raw scores overstate fraud probability by construction, before you even get to model-specific miscalibration.

What calibrated means

A calibrated model has scores that match observed frequencies: among all transactions scored around 0.80, roughly 80% are actually fraudulent. Once that holds, the score stops being an internal confidence number and becomes something you can plug into expected value math.

The pipeline

  1. Measure calibration. Bin predictions, compare predicted probability to observed fraud rate per bin. Reliability curves make it visual; Expected Calibration Error (ECE) gives one number.
  2. Calibrate. Fit Platt scaling (a logistic on the scores) or isotonic regression (monotonic, non-parametric, needs more data) on a holdout set the model never trained on. Calibrating on training data just memorizes the miscalibration.
  3. Convert to money. Expected loss = P(fraud) * transaction exposure. A calibrated 0.60 on $10,000 is $6,000 of expected loss.
  4. Compare against the cost of the alternative. Blocking a legitimate customer has its own cost: lost revenue, support load, churn. Block when expected loss exceeds that cost.
score -> calibrated P(fraud) -> expected loss -> action

The naive version is score -> block above threshold. The threshold version can still work for ranking-only use, but it cannot adapt to transaction size. A 0.95 on a $10 transaction and a 0.60 on a $50,000 transaction are opposite decisions once you price them.

Back to the 60% question

If only 60% of transactions scored 0.95 are fraud, the honest answer is: the model over-reports, so calibrate it, then redo the math with 0.60. Expected loss drops from $9,500 to $6,000. That may still clear the bar for blocking a $10,000 transaction, but now the decision has a defensible number under it instead of a confidence score dressed up as one.
