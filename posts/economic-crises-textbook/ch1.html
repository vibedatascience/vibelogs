Chapter 1

What Turns a Shock into a Crisis?

The anatomy of 2008, the dash for cash in 2020, and the run on Silicon Valley Bank

A shock is not yet a crisis. A fall in house prices, a pandemic, or a rise in interest rates becomes systemic only when the financial system transmits and magnifies it. This chapter develops a vocabulary for that transformation. Three episodes supply the evidence; seven influential readings show why economists still disagree about diagnosis, policy, and prevention.

Learning objectives
By the end of the chapter, you should be able to:
  1. distinguish a shock from the mechanisms that amplify it;
  2. separate liquidity problems from solvency problems without pretending that the distinction is always observable in real time;
  3. trace a crisis from asset losses through funding stress, fire sales, credit contraction, and real economic damage;
  4. compare the Global Financial Crisis, March 2020, and SVB using a common balance-sheet framework; and
  5. explain why emergency stabilization and long-run reform pose different policy questions.

1.1 A grammar of financial crisis

Financial crises look chaotic because many prices and institutions move at once. Their logic is easier to see if we separate three layers: the initiating shock, the financial amplification mechanism, and the real consequences.

Definition — Financial crisis. A disturbance becomes a financial crisis when losses, uncertainty, or withdrawals impair the financial system's ability to fund itself, make markets, allocate credit, or provide payments, and the resulting disruption threatens activity beyond the institutions initially hit. This is an economic definition, not a claim that every crisis follows one legal or statistical threshold.

Suppose an intermediary owns long-duration assets worth A, financed by equity E and runnable or short-term debt D, so that A = E + D. Leverage is A/E. An asset loss is absorbed first by equity. The same asset loss therefore produces a much larger percentage loss of equity when E is small. If creditors respond by refusing to roll over D, the intermediary must find cash, borrow against collateral, or sell assets. Those sales can depress the very prices used to measure its net worth.

Shock or reassessment→Net-worth loss→ Funding pressure→Fire sales and tighter credit→ Lower spending and output

The arrows are not mechanical laws. They are contingencies. Deep markets, long-maturity funding, capital buffers, deposit insurance, and credible central-bank facilities can break the chain. Leverage, opaque assets, concentrated uninsured funding, and common exposures can strengthen it. The central problem of crisis economics is to understand when private attempts to become safer—selling an asset, calling a loan, hoarding cash—make the system less safe.

Liquidity and solvency. A borrower is illiquid when it cannot meet payments as they come due without selling assets at distressed prices; it is insolvent when the value of its assets is below the value of its liabilities. The textbook distinction is sharp. The real-time distinction is not: forced sales can turn illiquidity into insolvency, and uncertainty about asset values can make a solvent institution unable to borrow.

Four mechanisms to watch

Leverage concentrates asset losses in a thin equity cushion. Maturity and liquidity transformation finances assets that are slow to sell with claims redeemable quickly. Coordination makes withdrawal individually rational when claimholders expect others to withdraw. Network and price spillovers transmit distress through direct obligations, common portfolios, and fire-sale prices. Later chapters model these mechanisms separately. In an actual crisis, several usually operate together.

How to read the evidence
A chronology can establish sequence, but sequence alone does not identify causation. The figures in this chapter are stylized reconstructions of lecture charts, not a replication dataset. Read them as disciplined prompts: What changed? Through which balance sheet? What alternative explanation fits the same pattern? The accompanying readings supply arguments and evidence, sometimes in direct disagreement.

1.2 The Global Financial Crisis

The crisis did not begin with Lehman Brothers. Falling house prices exposed losses on mortgages and mortgage-linked securities; uncertainty about who held those losses made short-term lenders demand more protection or withdraw; institutions responded by shrinking balance sheets; and lower market prices generated further measured losses. By September 2008 the system had already experienced the failure of major mortgage lenders and the rescue of Bear Stearns. Lehman's bankruptcy on September 15 was an accelerant inside an existing crisis, not its original cause.

Lehman was a broker-dealer funded heavily in wholesale markets rather than a deposit-funded commercial bank. Its assets were difficult to value and its ability to operate depended on creditors and counterparties continuing to transact. When that confidence disappeared, liquidity and solvency could no longer be cleanly separated. Could the Federal Reserve legally and practically have prevented bankruptcy? Lawrence Ball's detailed reconstruction answers yes and argues that officials chose not to lend; official accounts have emphasized inadequate collateral and legal constraints. For the purposes of this chapter, the disagreement is itself instructive: in a panic, the diagnosis that determines intervention is made under severe uncertainty, and that diagnosis can alter the outcome.

The propagation chain

The next figures organize the evidence as a propagation chain: repricing and uncertainty, tighter lending standards, contracting credit, deteriorating loan performance, household deleveraging, and persistent labor-market and output losses. The ordering is useful, but it is not a one-way causal estimate. Defaults weaken banks while weak banks restrict borrowers; falling activity worsens asset quality while credit contraction depresses activity. Crisis amplification is a feedback system.

The distinction in that caption matters. GDP that was never produced cannot be counted directly; it must be estimated against a path that might have occurred. The Dallas Fed authors therefore report a range rather than false precision. They also document losses outside GDP: household net worth fell by $16 trillion from 2007:Q3 to 2009:Q1, payroll employment fell by 8.7 million during the recession, and prolonged unemployment damaged skills, security, and well-being. A crisis is costly not merely because asset owners lose wealth, but because disrupted finance changes employment, investment, consumption, and opportunity.

…despite enormous policy intervention

Emergency policy had at least three distinct jobs: arrest runs and restore market functioning; recapitalize or resolve damaged institutions; and support aggregate demand after private spending collapsed. These jobs require different tools. The Fed cut its policy rate from 5.25% to nearly zero, created liquidity facilities, and expanded its balance sheet. Fiscal authorities guaranteed liabilities, injected capital, and used spending and tax measures. Similar interventions appeared abroad. The size of the response is evidence of the disruption's severity, not by itself proof that every program was well designed.

Stabilization is not recovery
A central bank can exchange liquid claims for eligible collateral and can influence financial conditions. It cannot, by that operation alone, restore lost borrower net worth, allocate fiscal losses, repair damaged institutions, or guarantee that newly liquid intermediaries will lend. Ending a panic and reversing its real consequences are related but different tasks.
The literature after 2008
Luttrell, Atkinson, and Rosenblum organize the crisis's cost across lost output, employment, wealth, and public intervention. Caballero argues that the crisis exposed the danger of treating highly stylized macroeconomic models as sufficiently precise guides to complex systemic events. Blanchard, Dell'Ariccia, and Mauro ask which pre-crisis policy doctrines survived and argue for a broader macroeconomic policy toolkit. These are complementary questions: How large was the damage? Why did prevailing frameworks miss the danger? What should policy do differently?

1.3 March 2020: a real shock becomes a dash for cash

The pandemic began outside finance. The abrupt stop in activity nevertheless produced an extraordinary demand for cash: firms drew credit lines, investors redeemed fund shares, and leveraged holders sold assets to meet margins. Even the US Treasury market showed signs of impaired intermediation. This was not a controlled experiment—the pandemic, private responses, and policy changed simultaneously—but it is a revealing comparison. A non-financial shock encountered balance sheets built for normal liquidity needs and nearly activated familiar amplification mechanisms.

The counterattack: policy as a circuit breaker

The response was unusually fast and broad. The lecture freezes a single day—March 18, 2020—as a specimen. On that one day, worldwide: the Bank of England expanded its bond-purchase program by £200 billion and cut rates to 0.1%; the Fed established new dollar swap lines with additional central banks and launched a Money Market Mutual Fund Liquidity Facility; the Bank of Japan bought a record ¥201.6 billion of ETFs and offered ¥2 trillion in unscheduled lending; the Swiss National Bank stepped up interventions; ECB action produced the biggest one-day drop in European corporate credit risk in years — while the Philadelphia Fed factory index posted a record fall and jobless claims blew past forecasts.

Inference, with caution
Market functioning improved rapidly after forceful monetary and fiscal announcements, while employment recovered much more slowly. That pattern is consistent with policy breaking an adverse financial feedback loop. It does not show that every intervention was necessary or that later inflation had a single cause. Pandemic supply disruptions, reopening demand, fiscal support, monetary accommodation, and subsequent shocks all belong in that debate. The central lesson here is narrower: credible backstops can change financial behavior before the full resources are deployed.

1.4 Silicon Valley Bank: safe assets, unsafe funding

Silicon Valley Bank demonstrates that a bank does not need subprime mortgages to become fragile. During the technology boom, SVB received a surge of deposits and invested heavily in long-duration Treasury and agency securities. These assets carried little credit risk but substantial interest-rate risk: their market values fell when rates rose. Many were classified as held to maturity, which delayed recognition of market-value losses in regulatory measures but did not remove the economic loss.

The other side of the balance sheet was unusually run-prone. A large share of SVB's deposits exceeded the deposit-insurance limit and belonged to firms whose cash needs were correlated. As rates rose and venture funding slowed, deposits were already leaving. SVB then announced a securities sale and attempted capital raise; the announcement revealed both the loss and the need for cash. Depositors requested about $42 billion on March 9, 2023, and regulators closed the bank the next day. Digital banking accelerated the run, but technology was an accelerant rather than the underlying vulnerability.

A resemblance, not a single recipe
2008: losses on housing credit interacted with leverage and wholesale funding. 2020: an external shutdown produced a broad dash for cash and stressed market intermediation. 2023: interest-rate losses met concentrated, uninsured deposits. All three involved demands for liquidity and balance sheets unable to adjust instantly. But the initiating shocks, assets, institutions, and policy problems differed. Analogy helps only when it preserves those differences.

1.5 Why crises rhyme—but do not repeat

Calomiris and Jaremski study ten crises spanning two millennia and reject both extremes: treating every crisis as unique teaches too little, while forcing every episode into one universal story erases important causes. Their preferred method is a taxonomy: compare episodes across a stable set of questions, then group similar mechanisms without claiming that one narrative fits all history.

Their survey highlights four recurring reasons why risk may be suddenly repriced. The categories are deliberately plain:

  1. Political incentives. Policies can subsidize risks that benefit influential borrowers, intermediaries, or constituencies while shifting losses to others.
  2. Changes in risk appetite or market conditions. Interest rates, wealth, beliefs, and tolerance for risk can change the prices investors are willing to pay.
  3. Learning about novelty. New products, technologies, and markets create opportunities precisely because their risks are not yet well understood.
  4. Hidden losses and fraud. Once trouble begins, insiders may conceal losses. In their taxonomy, fraud often magnifies or delays recognition of distress rather than serving as the universal first cause.

This framework explains why prevention is difficult. Some risks accompany genuine innovation; some serve powerful political coalitions; some are attractive privately because losses can be shifted; and some cannot be measured until experience arrives. The lesson is not fatalism. It is humility about promises to eliminate crises and discipline about matching reforms to mechanisms.

The chapter's central idea
A crisis is not defined by the asset that falls. It is defined by the system's response: who bears the loss, who needs cash, which promises can run, what must be sold, and whether private attempts to become safe transmit damage to everyone else.

The methodology

Each later chapter introduces one friction at a time: limited pledgeability, collateral constraints, sequential service, missing insurance, or a lower bound on interest rates. This is not a claim that the world contains one friction at a time. Simple models are diagnostic tools. Caballero's warning about a “pretense of knowledge” is especially relevant here: precision inside a model should not be confused with precise knowledge of a complex financial system. The discipline is to state what a model leaves out, compare its mechanism with evidence, and avoid asking one model to answer every policy question.

1.7 Summary

  • A shock becomes a financial crisis when balance-sheet responses impair funding, market functioning, payments, or credit and spread damage beyond the original loss.
  • Leverage magnifies losses to equity; runnable funding turns doubts about value into urgent demands for cash; forced sales can make both problems worse.
  • Liquidity and solvency are distinct concepts but interact in real time. A forced liquidation can destroy value, while uncertainty about value can close funding.
  • The Global Financial Crisis connected housing losses to wholesale runs, fire sales, a credit contraction, and lasting real costs.
  • March 2020 showed that an external real shock can generate a financial dash for cash. Fast, credible backstops helped interrupt that feedback loop.
  • SVB paired low-credit-risk assets with severe duration risk and unusually runnable deposits. “Safe assets” do not guarantee a safe balance sheet.
  • History offers recurring mechanisms, not one universal crisis script. Good analysis combines comparison, institutional detail, and simple models.

1.8 Questions for study

  1. A bank has assets of 100, debt of 92, and equity of 8. Its assets lose 4% of their value. By what percentage does its equity fall? What does this illustrate?
  2. Give one example in which illiquidity can create insolvency and one in which suspected insolvency can create illiquidity.
  3. Trace one feedback loop in the 2008 evidence using at least three figures. Which direction of causality is uncertain?
  4. Why is the Dallas Fed's $6–14 trillion estimate a range? Name two counterfactual assumptions needed to estimate output that was never produced.
  5. Compare March 2020 and SVB. Who wanted cash, what could be sold or pledged, and which policy commitment interrupted the run?
  6. Would full deposit insurance eliminate bank crises? Discuss the benefit to run prevention and the possible effect on incentives before a crisis.
  7. Choose a historical crisis not discussed here. Classify it using Calomiris and Jaremski's four recurring categories. Where does the taxonomy fit poorly?
  8. Ball and the Federal Reserve's official account disagree about whether Lehman could have been supported. What evidence would distinguish legal incapacity, insufficient collateral, and a discretionary policy choice?

The course lists no starred required reading for Lecture 1; these are complementary perspectives. They should not be treated as a consensus statement.

  1. Luttrell, David, Tyler Atkinson, and Harvey Rosenblum (2013), “Assessing the Costs and Consequences of the 2007–09 Financial Crisis and Its Aftermath.” Use for: the scale and uncertainty of output, employment, wealth, and well-being losses.
  2. Allen, Franklin, Elena Carletti, Jan Pieter Krahnen, and Marcel Tyrell (2011), “An Introduction to Liquidity and Crises,” in Liquidity and Crises. Use for: the institutional and theoretical relationship between liquidity, runs, contagion, and crisis management.

  4. Caballero, Ricardo J. (2010), “Macroeconomics after the Crisis: Time to Deal with the Pretense-of-Knowledge Syndrome.” Use for: methodological humility and the limits of fine-tuning complex systems with overly stylized models.
  5. Blanchard, Olivier, Giovanni Dell'Ariccia, and Paolo Mauro (2010), “Rethinking Macroeconomic Policy.” Use for: reconsidering the pre-crisis division among monetary, fiscal, and regulatory policy.
  6. Caballero, Ricardo J. (2009), “Crisis and Reform: Managing Systemic Risk.” Use for: the distinction between managing systemic panic and designing longer-run reform.
  7. Calomiris, Charles W., and Matthew S. Jaremski (2024), “The Puzzling Persistence of Financial Crises,” NBER Working Paper 32213. Use for: historical taxonomy and why crises recur. The OCW reading list says 32212; the paper itself is 32213.
