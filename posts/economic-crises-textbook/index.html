Economic Crises

Financial frictions, amplification, runs, bubbles, and the policies that fight them.
Based on MIT 14.454 (Spring 2026), taught by Ricardo J. Caballero.

Every generation believes it has outgrown financial panics, and every generation is wrong. The names change — country banks in 1907, broker-dealers in 2008, regional banks and stablecoins in 2023, private-credit funds in 2026 — but the recipe barely does: long-term, illiquid, risky assets funded by short-term, confidence-sensitive claims, levered up in good times and run upon in bad ones. This book is about that recipe. It develops, one chapter at a time, the canonical models economists use to understand financial crises: how small shocks are amplified by collateral and net worth, why bank capital matters, why runs are self-fulfilling and contagious, where bubbles come from and when they are useful, why credit booms can be inefficient even with fully rational agents, how deleveraging drags economies into liquidity traps, and why capital flows stop suddenly.

The method is deliberately spare. Each chapter takes one or two workhorse models, strips them to the minimal set of assumptions that produce the crisis mechanism, works the algebra, and then holds the model up against a real episode — the Global Financial Crisis, COVID-19, Silicon Valley Bank, the Asian crisis, the AI investment boom. Diagrams and charts carry as much weight as equations; nearly every figure from the original lecture slides is reproduced here in a redrawn, annotated form.

Contents

  • Chapter 1
    What Turns a Shock into a Crisis?
    A readable framework for leverage, liquidity, runs, and amplification; comparative studies of 2008, March 2020, and SVB; a guide to the Lecture 1 literature; summaries and study questions. 27 figures.
  • Chapter 2
    Collateral and Amplification
    Bernanke–Gertler (1989): net worth and agency costs. Kiyotaki–Moore (1997): credit cycles and the land-price multiplier. In preparation.
  • Chapter 3
    The Credit Crunch
    Holmström–Tirole (1997): firm, bank, and investor capital; flight to quality; credit crunch vs. collateral squeeze vs. savings squeeze. In preparation.
  • Chapter 4
    Runs, Panics, and Contagion
    Diamond–Dybvig (1983); the repo run of 2007–08; Allen–Gale (2000) network contagion; fire sales under complexity (Caballero–Simsek 2013). In preparation.
  • Chapter 5
    Bubbles and Speculation
    Rational bubbles (Samuelson–Tirole); risk shifting (Allen–Gale); riding the bubble (Abreu–Brunnermeier); disagreement and the resale option (Harrison–Kreps); speculative growth and the AI boom. In preparation.
  • Chapter 6
    Credit Booms
    Martin–Ventura: bubbly collateral and crowding in. Lorenzoni (2008): fire-sale pecuniary externalities and macroprudential policy. In preparation.
  • Chapter 7
    The Liquidity Trap and Leverage
    Korinek–Simsek (2016): deleveraging at the zero lower bound, aggregate-demand externalities, and the case for debt limits. In preparation.
  • Chapter 8
    Capital Flows and Sudden Stops
    Caballero–Krishnamurthy (2006): bubbles and capital-flow volatility. Bianchi–Lorenzoni (2022): capital controls and reserves. From EM crises to the GFC. In preparation.
  • Chapter 9
    Financial Conditions Guidance
    The recruitment effect: how central banks steer the economy through financial markets. In preparation.
  • Appendix
    Problems
    Ten long problems keyed to the chapters, from Bernanke–Gertler to bubbles and capital-flow volatility. In preparation.
